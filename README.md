## Executive summary

The human brain is a living, adaptive information-processing organ embedded in a body and environment. It contains neurons, glia, blood vessels, immune-related cells, and complex chemical and electrical signaling systems. No single region does all the work: most abilities arise from interacting circuits whose contributions vary with task, context, development, and state.

There is strong evidence that brain activity is necessary for ordinary human perception, thought, emotion, memory, action, and wakeful conscious experience. Damage, drugs, anesthesia, sleep, stimulation, and disease can predictably change these functions. Yet neuroscience has not fully explained **why and how physical activity is accompanied by subjective experience**—the felt quality of seeing red, being in pain, or having a thought. Researchers can identify neural correlates of conscious states and test theories, but there is no universally accepted, complete theory of consciousness.

The field is strongest at describing anatomy, cell types, circuits, measurable signals, and many causal links between brain systems and behavior. It is less complete at explaining high-level cognition from first principles, predicting an individual's future mental state, and turning discoveries into durable treatments for complex disorders. A major modern strategy is to connect molecular cell atlases, wiring diagrams, large-scale activity recordings, computational models, and carefully controlled human studies.

Treatments range from sleep, rehabilitation, psychotherapy, and medicines to surgery, implanted stimulation, noninvasive brain stimulation, and experimental brain–computer interfaces. Every intervention has a context-dependent benefit–risk balance. "Improving the brain" is not one measurable goal: stronger attention, less anxiety, better memory, creativity, emotional stability, and reduced pain may trade off against one another.

## 1. What the brain is

The brain is the central organ of the nervous system. It works with the spinal cord, peripheral nerves, endocrine system, immune system, sensory organs, muscles, and internal organs. It is not an isolated computer: its operation depends on oxygen, glucose, blood flow, body temperature, hormones, sleep, and continuous feedback from the body.

### Major structures and their broad roles

| Structure or system | Well-supported functions | Connection to consciousness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cerebral cortex** | Perception, language, planning, working memory, flexible action, and many forms of abstract reasoning | Different cortical networks represent contents of experience; the exact circuits sufficient for consciousness remain debated. |
| **Thalamus** | Routes and coordinates much sensory information; participates in attention, arousal, and cortical communication | Thalamocortical loops are important for conscious level and sensory experience. |
| **Brainstem** | Vital functions, sleep–wake regulation, arousal systems, reflexes, and basic motor coordination | Damage to key arousal systems can impair wakefulness; consciousness is not simply a property of cortex alone. |
| **Cerebellum** | Coordination, timing, motor learning, prediction, and contributions to some cognitive processes | It has enormous numbers of neurons, but its role in conscious experience is less direct and remains an active question. |
| **Basal ganglia** | Action selection, movement, habit learning, reward-related learning | Affect which actions and goals are selected; not generally treated as a standalone generator of experience. |
| **Hippocampus and medial temporal lobe** | Formation of many new episodic memories and spatial/contextual learning | Memory shapes the continuity and content of experience, but remembering and being conscious are not identical. |
| **Hypothalamus and related systems** | Homeostasis, hunger, thirst, temperature, circadian rhythms, endocrine regulation | Bodily drives and internal state influence mood, attention, and what becomes salient. |
| **Amygdala and connected networks** | Learning about biologically significant events, threat, reward, and emotional relevance | Emotion changes conscious appraisal and attention; the amygdala is not simply a "fear center." |
| **White matter** | Long-range communication through axons | Disrupted connectivity can alter integration of information and conscious responsiveness. |

These are useful summaries, not rigid one-function-per-region labels. Most regions contribute to multiple functions, and most complex functions depend on networks spanning several regions. Source: [NINDS, Brain Basics](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics).

### Neurons, glia, and synapses

- **Neurons** receive, transform, and transmit signals. Dendrites and cell bodies integrate inputs; axons carry electrical impulses; synapses transmit signals to other cells.
- **Action potentials** are rapid changes in membrane voltage that propagate along axons. Their timing and rate can encode information, but neural codes differ by circuit and task.
- **Chemical synapses** release neurotransmitters that change the probability that a target cell will become active. Electrical synapses also exist.
- **Excitation and inhibition** are both essential. Healthy function depends on their balance and timing, not simply on "more activity."
- **Glial cells** are active participants, not mere packing material. Astrocytes support metabolism and regulate the extracellular environment; oligodendrocytes form myelin; microglia participate in immune surveillance and synapse remodeling.
- **Neuromodulators** such as dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, and various peptides can change learning, motivation, arousal, and how circuits respond. It is misleading to reduce them to single labels such as "pleasure chemical." See [Section 2A](#2a-neurotransmitters-of-the-human-brain) for a transmitter-by-transmitter guide and [Section 2B](#2b-important-proteins-of-the-human-brain) for key brain proteins.
- **Plasticity** means that neural function and connections can change with experience, learning, injury, and development. Plasticity supports recovery and learning, but it can also reinforce maladaptive habits, chronic pain, addiction, or post-traumatic responses.

For a cell-by-cell guide to the main neuron, glial, vascular, and immune cell types and where each is found, see [Section 2](#2-the-cell-types-of-the-human-brain).

**Consciousness relevance:** conscious contents depend on neural activity, but there is no simple equation such as "one neuron equals one thought." Researchers investigate how distributed activity becomes coordinated, how information is selected and maintained, and which activity patterns distinguish conscious from unconscious processing.

## 2. The cell types of the human brain

Section 1 introduced neurons and glia as broad categories. This section opens those categories up: it lists the main cell types found in the human brain, what each one does, and where it is found. It is a guide to the cellular "parts list," not a complete catalog. Modern cell atlases divide the brain into hundreds of clusters and thousands of subclusters, and where one type ends and the next begins depends partly on the criteria used. The chemical messengers these cells release and the key proteins they depend on are covered in Sections 2A and 2B.

**How to read the tables**
- **Where found** gives the main locations. Most cell types also occur elsewhere at lower density.
- **Main functions** lists well-supported roles. Some are inferred from animal studies, which the caveats column flags; others come from human lesion, recording, imaging, and postmortem work.
- No cell type works alone. Function emerges from interactions among cell types within circuits, as described in the multilevel view in Section 3.
- These are educational summaries. They are not a basis for inferring anything about a particular person's brain.

### How many cells, and how are "types" defined?

- **Counts.** One widely cited count of four adult male brains found about 86 billion neurons and about 85 billion non-neuronal cells. "Non-neuronal" includes glia but also vascular and other cells, so it is not a synonym for glia. The older textbook claim of ten glia per neuron is not supported by modern counts. Numbers differ across individuals, ages, and counting methods.
- **Regional variation.** The overall balance hides large regional differences. The cerebral cortex holds only about a fifth of all neurons (roughly 16 billion) and has several non-neuronal cells per neuron, while the cerebellum holds about four in five of all neurons and relatively few non-neuronal cells.
- **What defines a type.** Neuroscientists combine several criteria: gene expression, shape, electrical behavior, neurotransmitter, connections, location, and developmental origin. These criteria often agree, but not always, and some differences look like sharp categories while others look like gradients.
- **The current census.** A landmark 2023 single-nucleus RNA-sequencing census of the adult human brain analyzed about 3.4 million nuclei from roughly 100 dissections of three donors and grouped them into 31 superclusters, 461 clusters, and 3,313 subclusters. Neuron diversity was especially high in the midbrain and hindbrain, and astrocytes and oligodendrocyte-lineage cells also varied by region. A cluster is a statistical grouping by gene expression; it is not automatically a distinct functional cell type, and three donors cannot capture the full range of individual variation.

### Neurons: major types and where they are found

Neurons can be sorted by several criteria at once: whether they excite, inhibit, or modulate their targets; whether their main role is sensory, relay, local processing, or motor output; their shape; and their location. The table mixes these criteria because that is how the names are actually used.

| Cell type | Main functions | Where found | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pyramidal neurons** | Excitatory (glutamate-releasing) principal neurons. They collect inputs on branching dendrites, including a long apical dendrite, and send output over long distances to other cortical areas, the striatum, thalamus, brainstem, or spinal cord, depending on subtype. | Cerebral cortex (layers II–VI, especially III and V), hippocampus (CA1–CA3), and amygdala. | A family of many subtypes defined by layer, projection target, and gene expression, not a single cell type. Most cortical neurons are excitatory. |
| **Betz cells (giant pyramidal neurons)** | Very large layer V neurons whose axons descend in the corticospinal tract. They are part of the upper motor neuron pathway that drives voluntary movement. | Primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus). | Only a small minority of corticospinal fibers come from Betz cells; other pyramidal neurons contribute too. |
| **GABAergic interneurons** (e.g., basket, chandelier, and Martinotti cells) | Release GABA to inhibit neighboring cells. They shape timing, gain control, rhythmic activity, and the balance between excitation and inhibition. Different subtypes target different parts of a pyramidal neuron. | Throughout cortex and hippocampus; related inhibitory neurons occur in the thalamus, striatum, and brainstem. | A minority of cortical neurons but highly diverse. Marker-based classes (parvalbumin, somatostatin, VIP) are useful groupings, but a marker does not map one-to-one onto a function. Disrupted inhibition is implicated in some seizure disorders. |
| **Von Economo neurons (spindle neurons)** | Large, elongated projection neurons proposed to relay information rapidly within networks involved in social and emotional processing and awareness of bodily state. | Mainly layer V of the anterior cingulate and frontoinsular cortex; found in humans and great apes and in some other large-brained species such as whales and elephants. | Function is hypothesized, not established. Loss has been reported early in the behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia. |
| **Dentate granule cells** | Small excitatory neurons that receive input from the entorhinal cortex and pass it on to hippocampal area CA3. Often proposed to help separate similar memories ("pattern separation"). | Dentate gyrus of the hippocampus. | Pattern separation is a leading but not the only interpretation, and much of the evidence comes from rodents. Adult neurogenesis here is debated (see the progenitor table below). |
| **Place, grid, and concept cells** (functional classes) | Neurons defined by what they respond to: place cells to particular locations, grid cells to regular lattice-like spatial patterns, and cells in the human medial temporal lobe to particular people or concepts. | Hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and medial temporal lobe. | These are firing-property labels, not new anatomical types; the cells belong to classes such as pyramidal neurons. Place and grid cells were established in rodents; human single-neuron recordings, usually in patients undergoing clinically indicated monitoring, support similar cells. |
| **Thalamic relay (thalamocortical) neurons** | Excitatory neurons that carry sensory signals and signals from other brain areas to cortex. Their firing mode changes with arousal, helping gate what reaches cortex. | Thalamic nuclei, such as the lateral geniculate (vision), medial geniculate (hearing), ventral posterior nuclei (touch), and higher-order nuclei such as the pulvinar. | More than a passive relay: higher-order nuclei take part in loops between cortex and thalamus. Subtypes differ in how widely they project to cortex. |
| **Thalamic reticular neurons** | GABAergic neurons that inhibit thalamic relay cells. They take part in sleep spindles and are thought to help regulate attention-related gating. | Thalamic reticular nucleus, a thin shell around the thalamus. | The sleep-spindle role is well supported; the attention-gating role is a leading hypothesis. |
| **Medium spiny (spiny projection) neurons** | GABAergic output neurons of the striatum, organized into "direct" and "indirect" pathways that help select, initiate, and suppress actions and learn from reward. | Striatum (caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens). | The direct/indirect division is a useful simplification; the pathways interact more than the names suggest. Preferentially lost in Huntington's disease. |
| **Purkinje cells** | Large GABAergic neurons that are the sole output of the cerebellar cortex. Each integrates a huge number of inputs; central to motor timing, coordination, and learning. | Purkinje cell layer of the cerebellar cortex. | Their flat, fan-like dendrites are distinctive. The cerebellum also contributes to some non-motor functions, but those roles are less well understood. |
| **Cerebellar granule cells** | Tiny excitatory neurons that receive input from mossy fibers and send parallel fibers to Purkinje cells. Thought to expand and recode incoming signals. | Granule layer of the cerebellar cortex. | Commonly estimated to make up more than half of all neurons in the brain, which is why the cerebellum holds about four in five of them. |
| **Motor neurons (upper and lower)** | Upper motor neurons (including Betz cells) originate in cortex and command lower motor neurons, which directly activate muscles. | Upper: motor and premotor cortex. Lower: cranial-nerve motor nuclei in the brainstem (face, jaw, tongue, eye movements) and the ventral horn of the spinal cord. | Spinal motor neurons lie outside the brain proper. Both upper and lower motor neurons degenerate in ALS. |
| **Sensory receptor and relay neurons** | Receptor cells convert physical energy into neural signals; relay and processing neurons pass those signals through brainstem and thalamus to cortex. | Receptors lie mostly outside the brain proper (cochlear hair cells, olfactory sensory neurons in the nose, touch and pain neurons in ganglia); the retina develops as part of the central nervous system. Relay and processing neurons are in brainstem nuclei, thalamus, olfactory bulb, and sensory cortex. | The boundary between "brain" and "periphery" is partly conventional. |

### Neuromodulatory and homeostatic neuron groups

Small clusters of neurons in the brainstem, hypothalamus, and basal forebrain send axons across large parts of the brain and release modulators that change how other circuits behave. They are the cellular side of the neuromodulators mentioned in Section 1.

| Neuron group | Main functions | Where found | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dopaminergic neurons** | Substantia nigra neurons project to the striatum and support movement and habit learning. Ventral tegmental area neurons project to the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex and are linked to reward prediction, motivation, and learning. | Midbrain (substantia nigra pars compacta and ventral tegmental area). | Dopamine is not simply a "pleasure chemical." Loss of substantia nigra neurons is central to Parkinson's disease. |
| **Serotonergic neurons** | Broadly modulate mood, arousal, sleep, appetite, pain, and learning. | Raphe nuclei along the brainstem midline, with projections across the brain. | Effects depend on the receptor type and the target region. "Low serotonin causes depression" is an oversimplification. |
| **Noradrenergic neurons** | Support arousal, vigilance, attention, and stress responses. | Locus coeruleus in the pons. | A very small nucleus with brain-wide projections. |
| **Cholinergic neurons** | Support attention, learning, cortical arousal, and REM sleep. | Basal forebrain (including the nucleus basalis of Meynert), which projects to cortex and hippocampus; and brainstem nuclei such as the pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei. | Basal forebrain cholinergic neurons are affected in Alzheimer's disease, and some symptomatic Alzheimer's medicines act on acetylcholine signaling (Section 7). |
| **Histaminergic neurons** | Promote wakefulness. | Tuberomammillary nucleus of the hypothalamus. | Part of why some antihistamines are sedating. |
| **Orexin (hypocretin) neurons** | Stabilize wakefulness and link arousal to feeding and energy state. | Lateral hypothalamus. | Loss of these neurons underlies narcolepsy type 1. |
| **Hypothalamic homeostatic and neurosecretory neurons** | Regulate hunger and satiety, temperature, thirst, and hormone release. Examples include arcuate-nucleus neurons that promote or suppress feeding, and oxytocin and vasopressin neurons that signal to the pituitary. | Hypothalamus. | Much of the cell-level detail comes from animal studies. |

### Glial cells: types, functions, and locations

Glia are active partners of neurons, not packing material. They differ in origin and in function.

| Cell type | Main functions | Where found | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Astrocytes** | Take up neurotransmitters such as glutamate and excess potassium, provide metabolic support, help shape and remove synapses, and send "endfeet" to blood vessels, where they help maintain the blood–brain barrier and couple neural activity to blood flow. | Throughout the brain and spinal cord; protoplasmic astrocytes in gray matter and fibrous astrocytes in white matter. | Human astrocytes have been reported to be larger and structurally more complex than rodent astrocytes, with some forms found only in primates. The role of astrocyte calcium signaling in information processing is debated. |
| **Oligodendrocytes** | Wrap central nervous system axons in myelin, which speeds conduction and reduces energy cost. One cell supports several axon segments, and oligodendrocytes also provide metabolic support to axons. | Mostly white matter, but also gray matter. Myelination continues from infancy through adolescence into early adulthood in many regions. | Schwann cells, not oligodendrocytes, myelinate peripheral nerves. Myelin can be remodeled with experience, with stronger evidence in animals than in humans. |
| **Oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs, or NG2 glia)** | Self-renewing precursors that generate new oligodendrocytes across life, supporting myelin formation, plasticity, and repair. Some receive synaptic input from neurons. | Gray and white matter throughout the central nervous system. | Their roles beyond being precursors are under investigation. Repair of myelin after damage, as in multiple sclerosis, is often incomplete. |
| **Microglia** | The resident immune cells of the brain. They survey tissue, clear debris and dying cells, prune synapses during development, and respond to injury, infection, and disease by releasing signaling molecules. | Throughout the brain and spinal cord, as a minority of brain cells. | They arise from early embryonic precursors and largely renew themselves (shown most clearly in mice). "Activated" is not one state, and microglia can be protective or harmful depending on context. Variants in microglia-related genes alter Alzheimer's disease risk. |
| **Ependymal cells and tanycytes** | Ciliated ependymal cells line the fluid-filled ventricles and the central canal, help circulate cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and form a barrier between CSF and brain tissue. Tanycytes are specialized ependymal-like cells that sense nutrients and hormones and relay signals to hypothalamic circuits. | Walls of the ventricles; tanycytes along the floor of the third ventricle in the hypothalamus. | Tanycyte functions are best characterized in rodents. |

### Vascular, barrier, and immune-interface cells

The brain's blood vessels and borders are built from specialized cells that belong in any cell inventory. Together with astrocyte endfeet and neurons, vascular cells form the "neurovascular unit."

| Cell type | Main functions | Where found | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Endothelial cells** | Line the brain's capillaries. Tight junctions and specialized transporters form the blood–brain barrier, controlling what passes from blood into brain tissue. | Throughout the brain's blood vessels. | The barrier is selective, not absolute. Injury, inflammation, and disease can change it, and it complicates drug delivery to the brain. |
| **Pericytes** | Wrap capillaries; support barrier integrity and vessel stability and help regulate blood flow. | Along brain capillaries. | How much capillary pericytes actively control blood flow, compared with the smooth muscle of arterioles, is debated. |
| **Vascular smooth muscle cells** | Form the muscular wall of arteries and arterioles and adjust vessel diameter and local blood flow. | Arteries and arterioles on the brain surface and within brain tissue. | Vessel-wall changes contribute to vascular disorders such as stroke and vascular cognitive impairment. |
| **Choroid plexus epithelial cells** | Produce much of the cerebrospinal fluid and form the barrier between blood and CSF. | Choroid plexus of the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles. | A barrier and a signaling interface as well as a fluid source. |
| **Border-associated macrophages** | Immune cells that patrol the brain's borders and respond to pathogens and injury. | Around blood vessels (perivascular spaces), in the meninges, and in the choroid plexus. | Related to, but distinct from, microglia in location and in some properties; their roles are an active research area. |
| **Meningeal cells and fibroblasts** | Form the dura, arachnoid, and pia, the protective membranes that enclose the brain and the CSF-filled space around it. | Surface of the brain and spinal cord. | The meninges also host blood vessels and immune cells. |
| **Other immune cells (e.g., T cells)** | Few in healthy brain tissue; more numerous in infection, autoimmune, and inflammatory disease. | Mainly meninges, choroid plexus, and perivascular spaces. | The brain's "immune privilege" is relative, not total isolation from the immune system. |

| Cell type | Main functions | Where found | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Radial glia and neural progenitors (developing brain)** | Divide to produce neurons and glia, and their long fibers guide migrating neurons to their final positions. | Ventricular and subventricular zones of the developing brain. | The human fetal brain has an especially large population of "outer" radial glia, thought to contribute to cortical expansion. |

### Where cell types meet disease

Cell types help explain why diseases have the patterns they do. The table gives examples, not complete explanations.

| Cell group | Condition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra | Parkinson's disease | Progressive loss disrupts basal ganglia circuits and movement. Treatments such as levodopa and stimulation address symptoms rather than the underlying degeneration (Section 7). |
| Medium spiny neurons of the striatum | Huntington's disease | Preferentially lost, disrupting action selection and movement control. |
| Upper and lower motor neurons | Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) | Degeneration of both groups weakens muscles, including those for speech, swallowing, and breathing. |
| Oligodendrocytes and myelin | Multiple sclerosis | Immune-mediated damage to central nervous system myelin disrupts conduction along white matter pathways. |
| Orexin neurons | Narcolepsy type 1 | Loss of these neurons destabilizes wakefulness and sleep–wake transitions. |
| Von Economo neurons and related frontal and insular neurons | Behavioral-variant frontotemporal dementia | Loss has been reported early in the disease; whether it causes the symptoms is not established. |
| Cholinergic basal forebrain neurons, cortical and hippocampal neurons, microglia, and astrocytes | Alzheimer's disease | Many cell types are involved. Microglia-related genes alter risk, and some symptomatic medicines act on acetylcholine signaling. |
| Pyramidal neurons and inhibitory interneurons | Epilepsy | Abnormal synchronized activity reflects an imbalance in excitation, inhibition, or network dynamics; causes vary, and some forms involve changes in inhibitory interneuron function. |
| Neurons, glia, and vascular cells in the affected territory | Stroke | The brain's dependence on a continuous blood supply makes tissue vulnerable when a vessel is blocked or ruptured. |

A vulnerable cell type is not a full explanation of a disease. Genes, proteins, inflammation, blood vessels, circuits, and environment interact, as Section 6 emphasizes.

### How cell types work together: three examples

- **The synapse is more than two neurons.** Astrocyte processes enwrap many synapses and clear glutamate and potassium, and microglia help prune synapses during development. A synapse's behavior therefore depends on its cellular neighborhood.
- **The neurovascular unit links activity to blood flow.** Neurons, astrocytes, endothelial cells, pericytes, and smooth muscle cells coordinate blood-flow changes in response to local activity. This is why fMRI (Section 5) measures blood-oxygenation changes that are only indirectly related to neural firing, and why vascular problems can quickly affect function.
- **Myelinated pathways depend on glia.** Oligodendrocytes wrap axons in myelin, and oligodendrocyte precursor cells supply new ones. This supports the fast long-range communication of the white matter described in Section 1, and its failure helps explain demyelinating disease.

**Consciousness relevance:** cell types make the debates in Section 4 more concrete, but no cell type is established as the "generator" of experience.
- **Level of consciousness.** Wake-promoting neuromodulatory and hypothalamic neurons (noradrenergic, cholinergic, serotonergic, histaminergic, and orexin groups) and thalamocortical neurons help sustain wakefulness. This fits the structures table in Section 1, where damage to arousal systems or thalamocortical loops can impair wakefulness and conscious level.
- **Sleep and anesthesia.** Sleep spindles arise from interactions between thalamic reticular and thalamocortical neurons, and many anesthetics act on inhibitory GABA signaling among other targets. Cell-type-specific interactions are therefore part of how consciousness is lost and regained, as discussed in Section 3.
- **A cellular hypothesis.** In mice, three different anesthetics decoupled the dendrites of layer 5 pyramidal neurons from their cell bodies, disrupting feedback signaling (Suzuki and Larkum, 2020). The authors proposed this as a cellular mechanism linking anesthesia to theories that emphasize recurrent and thalamocortical interactions. It is a hypothesis supported by animal data, not an established human mechanism, and it does not settle the competition among the theories in Section 4.
- **Neuron number is not a consciousness meter.** The cerebellum holds about four in five of the brain's neurons, yet its link to experience is less direct. Theories differ in what they treat as the relevant feature; Integrated Information Theory, for example, emphasizes a system's causal structure rather than its cell count.
- **Glia shape the conditions for experience.** Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and vascular cells influence the ionic environment, conduction speed, and energy supply under which neurons work. Whether glia contribute more directly to experience is not established.

### What cell-type descriptions can and cannot tell us

- **Types are partly a modeling choice.** Cell categories are imposed on variation that is sometimes continuous. Different criteria, tissue sources, and statistical methods can split or merge types.
- **Function is often inferred.** Much of what is known about specific cell types comes from rodent and primate experiments. Human evidence comes from postmortem tissue, single-neuron recordings in patients, imaging, and genetics.
- **Cell-type vulnerability is a clue, not a full explanation.** Knowing which cells are lost in a disease does not by itself explain why they are lost or how to prevent it.
- **Counts and ratios vary.** Cell numbers differ by region, age, individual, and counting method.
- **No self-diagnosis.** Nothing here allows a person to infer the state of any particular cell population from symptoms, scores, or feelings.

## 2A. Neurotransmitters of the human brain

Section 1 introduced neurotransmitters and neuromodulators in passing, and Section 2 described the neurons that release them. This section lists the main neurotransmitters found in the human brain, what each one does, and what is generally associated with having too little or too much of its signaling. It is a guide to the chemical "vocabulary" of the brain, not a complete catalog: besides the molecules below, dozens of other neuropeptides (for example substance P, neuropeptide Y, and somatostatin) and gases such as nitric oxide also act as signals.

**How to read the tables**
- **Main functions** lists well-supported roles and the main cells or regions that release the transmitter. Roles differ by receptor type and brain region, so each summary is a simplification.
- **Effects of too little** describes what is reported or expected when signaling by that transmitter is reduced. "Too little" can come from lost neurons, reduced synthesis or release, faster removal, fewer receptors, or receptor blockade. The evidence ranges from well established (for example, loss of dopamine neurons in Parkinson's disease) to hypothesized or based mainly on animal studies; the caveats column flags which.
- **Effects of too much** describes what is reported or expected when signaling by that transmitter is excessive. "Too much" can come from more release, slower removal or breakdown, more or more-sensitive receptors, or drugs and poisons that force release or block removal. Most well-documented examples of excess come from drugs, overdoses, toxins, or inherited defects rather than from a simple surplus of the brain's own chemical, and evidence again ranges from well established to hypothesized.
- **Both extremes matter.** For most transmitters, performance follows an inverted U, with problems at both extremes.
- These are educational summaries. They are not a basis for inferring anything about a particular person's brain chemistry, and they are not medication or supplement advice.

### Fast amino-acid transmitters

These transmitters carry most of the brain's rapid point-to-point signaling.

| Neurotransmitter | Main functions | Effects of too little | Effects of too much | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Glutamate** (excitatory) | The brain's main excitatory transmitter and its most abundant one. Acts on NMDA, AMPA, kainate, and metabotropic receptors. Central to fast signaling, synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory. Released by pyramidal neurons, dentate granule cells, cerebellar granule cells, and thalamocortical neurons (Section 2). | Weaker excitatory drive and impaired synaptic plasticity, with learning and memory problems. Reduced NMDA-receptor function, as in anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis or with NMDA-blocking drugs such as ketamine, is associated with memory problems, psychosis-like or dissociative symptoms, seizures, and altered consciousness. | Excitotoxicity: excessive receptor activation, especially of NMDA receptors, lets in too much calcium and injures or kills neurons, as in stroke, traumatic brain injury, and prolonged seizures. Excess excitation also causes hyperexcitability and seizures. | **GABA** (gamma-aminobutyric acid; inhibitory) | The brain's main inhibitory transmitter. Released by GABAergic interneurons, Purkinje cells, medium spiny neurons, and thalamic reticular neurons. ### Monoamines, acetylcholine, and histamine

These transmitters are released by small brainstem, hypothalamic, and basal forebrain groups with brain-wide projections (see "Neuromodulatory and homeostatic neuron groups" in Section 2). They act mostly as modulators that change how other circuits respond.

| Neurotransmitter | Main functions | Effects of too little | Effects of too much | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dopamine** (catecholamine) | Substantia nigra neurons project to the striatum and support movement and habit learning. | **Acetylcholine** | Supports attention, learning, memory, cortical arousal, and REM sleep. Released by basal forebrain neurons that project to cortex and hippocampus and by brainstem nuclei. ### Peptides and other modulators

Peptide and lipid messengers are often released together with, or instead of, classical transmitters. They tend to act more slowly and more diffusely, and many act as hormones as well.

### What "too low" can and cannot mean for neurotransmitters

- **Signaling is more than a concentration.** The effect of a transmitter depends on synthesis, release, removal, receptor number and type, and the circuit it acts on. A system can be "low" in function even when the amount of the chemical is normal, and the reverse.
- **Brain levels cannot be read from the body.** Blood, urine, or saliva measurements do not reliably reflect what is happening in the brain, and consumer "neurotransmitter tests" have not been established as a basis for diagnosis or treatment.
- **Systems interact.** Glutamate and GABA are chemically linked, and dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, and acetylcholine influence one another. Changing one transmitter changes others, so single-chemical stories are usually incomplete.
- **Associations are not always causes.** Many reported links between a "low" transmitter and a condition come from animal studies, medication effects, or small human studies.
- **Common symptoms have many causes.** Low mood, poor attention, and sleep problems can arise from many sources, including medical illness, medications, sleep loss, stress, and life circumstances. Persistent or severe symptoms call for a qualified clinician, not self-diagnosis or self-treatment with supplements.
- **Medicines that act on these systems have benefits and risks** (Section 7). Nothing here shows that supplements marketed as "neurotransmitter support" correct a deficiency.

### What "too high" can and cannot mean for neurotransmitters

- **Excess usually comes from outside, or from a broken off-switch.** The brain normally regulates its transmitters tightly through feedback, reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, and receptor adjustment. Most documented "too much" states therefore come from drugs, overdoses, poisons, or inherited defects in breakdown or removal. Examples are serotonin syndrome from combined serotonin-raising medicines, cholinergic crisis from nerve agents, opioid overdose, and glycine buildup in nonketotic hyperglycinemia.
- **"High" has no single meaning.** Signaling can rise through more release, slower removal, more receptors, or more sensitive receptors. A system can also be excessive in one pathway and low in another, as in hypotheses that dopamine signaling is raised in some subcortical pathways and reduced in prefrontal cortex in schizophrenia.
- **The brain adapts.** Receptors change in number and sensitivity, so prolonged excess usually produces tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal rather than a stable "high" state. This is one reason stopping some drugs suddenly can be dangerous.
- **Brain levels still cannot be read from the body.** Blood, urine, and saliva results do not show how strongly a transmitter is signaling in the brain, so a consumer test reporting "high" levels is no basis for diagnosis or treatment.
- **Both extremes can look alike.** Anxiety, poor sleep, and poor attention can occur with too much or too little arousal signaling (the inverted U), so symptoms alone do not reveal which direction a system has moved.
- **Boosting is not a general strategy.** Nothing shows that supplements or "boosting" routines improve a healthy brain, and excess can cause harm. Medicines that raise a transmitter have specific uses and risks and belong under clinician guidance. Combining several medicines or supplements that act on the same system is a recognized cause of overdose-type reactions.
- **Some excess states are emergencies.** High fever with muscle rigidity and confusion after combining medicines, slowed breathing or unresponsiveness after opioids or sedatives, and confusion, seizures, and heavy secretions after a possible poisoning need urgent medical care (emergency services).
- **Associations are not always causes.** Reports linking high transmitter activity to a condition, such as hyperarousal in PTSD, often come from animal studies, medication effects, or small human studies.

**Consciousness relevance:** transmitter systems help set the level and contents of conscious experience without being its "generator."
- **Level of consciousness.** Noradrenergic, cholinergic, histaminergic, serotonergic, and orexin systems promote wakefulness (Section 2), and disturbing them changes alertness. Too much signaling can also impair awareness: excess GABA enhancement can cause sedation and coma, and excess glutamate activity can cause seizures that disrupt awareness.
- **Anesthesia and sedation.** Many anesthetics and sedatives enhance GABA signaling, and ketamine blocks NMDA-type glutamate receptors (anesthesia is discussed in Section 3). This links two of the major transmitter systems to the loss and recovery of responsiveness.
- **Altered contents of experience.** Drugs that activate certain serotonin receptors (such as the 5-HT2A receptor) change perception and thought, which shows that transmitter signaling shapes experience. No single transmitter is established as the source of experience, and the theories in Section 4 make different claims about what matters.

## 2B. Important proteins of the human brain

Cells and transmitters are built and controlled by proteins. Proteins form the receptors and channels that detect transmitters and generate electrical signals, the machinery that releases transmitters, the scaffolds that hold synapses together, the highways that carry cargo along axons, the insulation around axons, the transporters that feed the brain, and the switches that read genes during development. This section lists important proteins found in the human brain, what each one does, and what is associated with having too little or too much of it. It is a selected guide, not a complete catalog: the human brain expresses a very large share of the proteins encoded by the genome.

**How to read the tables**
- **Main functions** lists the well-supported normal role of each protein.
- **Effects of too much** describes what happens when the protein is present in extra copies, overproduced, overactive, or in an abnormal form that accumulates. Where the harm comes from a toxic form rather than from a larger amount of the normal protein, the entry says so.
- **Effects of too little** describes what happens when the protein is missing, reduced, or functionally lost. In humans this often comes from an inherited gene variant, autoantibodies that remove a protein, or loss of the cells that make it. Where evidence comes mainly from mice or other animals, the caveats column says so.
- Where a protein is best known for toxic gains of function (clumping or abnormal activity), the caveats column says so. Too little and too much, or normal and abnormal forms, can both cause disease.
- These are educational summaries. A gene variant or a protein measurement does not by itself predict what will happen in a given person.

### Synaptic signaling, receptors, and plasticity proteins

| Protein | Main functions | Effects of too little | Effects of too much | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NMDA receptors** (glutamate-gated ion channels) | Act as coincidence detectors that open when a transmitter is present and the cell is already active, allowing calcium entry that triggers synaptic strengthening. Central to learning and memory. | Loss-of-function variants in the genes for NMDA-receptor subunits (GRIN genes) are linked to intellectual disability and epilepsy. When autoantibodies remove receptors (anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis), people can develop psychiatric symptoms, memory loss, seizures, and reduced consciousness, which can improve with immune treatment. | Overactivation drives excitotoxicity in stroke, brain injury, and seizures. | **GABA-A receptors** (GABA-gated chloride channels) | Mediate fast inhibition. | **Voltage-gated sodium channels** (for example Nav1.1, encoded by SCN1A) | Generate the rising phase of the action potential. Nav1.1 is concentrated in inhibitory interneurons. | Loss of one working copy of SCN1A causes Dravet syndrome, a severe early-onset epilepsy with developmental problems. A leading explanation is that inhibitory interneurons fire less, so networks become over-excitable. | Gain-of-function variants cause hyperexcitability. SCN2A, SCN3A, and SCN8A variants of this kind cause early-onset epilepsies. In pain-sensing neurons, gain-of-function variants in SCN9A (Nav1.7) cause attacks of severe burning pain. | An example of how too little of a channel in inhibitory cells can cause too much network activity. Different sodium-channel genes are linked to different conditions. |
| **SNARE and release proteins** (SNAP-25, syntaxin-1, synaptobrevin, synaptotagmin, Munc18-1 or STXBP1) | Dock synaptic vesicles to the membrane, fuse them, and couple fusion to calcium entry, so that a transmitter is released when an action potential arrives. | Reduced release of transmitter. Loss of one working copy of STXBP1 causes an early-onset epileptic encephalopathy with developmental delay. Botulinum toxin cuts SNARE proteins at the neuromuscular junction and causes paralysis. | Unregulated release floods synapses with transmitter. Black widow spider venom (alpha-latrotoxin) triggers massive vesicle release and causes severe pain and muscle spasms. Release probability is tightly tuned, and too much release raises the risk of excitotoxicity. | The botulinum example is peripheral, outside the brain. Release proteins have many partners, and complete loss is often incompatible with life. |
| **CaMKII** (calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II) | An enzyme activated by calcium entry that helps strengthen synapses and is central to long-term potentiation. Abundant at excitatory synapses. | Mice lacking the alpha form show impaired long-term potentiation and spatial learning. Variants in CAMK2A and CAMK2B are linked to intellectual disability. | Gain-of-function variants in CAMK2A and CAMK2B are linked to intellectual disability, and some to epilepsy. Abnormally sustained CaMKII activation has been implicated in injury after ischemia. | Both loss and abnormally increased activity of variants can cause disease, so "too little" is only part of the picture. |
| **PSD-95** (encoded by DLG4) | A scaffold at the postsynaptic density that anchors receptors such as NMDA and AMPA receptors and links them to signaling proteins. | In animal studies, reduced PSD-95 gives weaker or immature synapses and impaired plasticity. DLG4 variants have been linked to neurodevelopmental disorders. | In cultured neurons and animals, overexpression enlarges synapses and adds AMPA receptors, and can reduce further plasticity. PSD-95 also couples NMDA receptors to nitric oxide production in excitotoxic injury, and drugs that uncouple this link have been tested in stroke. | One of many scaffold proteins. Its exact roles differ across synapse types. |
| **Neurexins and neuroligins** | Cell-adhesion proteins that span the synapse, helping form, specify, and stabilize connections between neurons. | Deletions or variants in genes such as NRXN1, NLGN3, and NLGN4 are associated with autism, intellectual disability, and increased risk for schizophrenia. | Copy-number gains in some of these genes have been reported in neurodevelopmental conditions. In animals, overexpressing neuroligins shifts the balance between excitatory and inhibitory synapses. | These are risk factors among many, and the same variant can lead to different outcomes in different people. |
| **Transmitter synthesis enzymes and transporters** (tyrosine hydroxylase, GAD65/67, choline acetyltransferase, and transporters such as DAT and VMAT2) | Tyrosine hydroxylase makes the precursor of dopamine and norepinephrine. GAD enzymes make GABA from glutamate. Choline acetyltransferase makes acetylcholine. DAT recycles dopamine, and VMAT2 packs monoamines into vesicles. | Tyrosine hydroxylase deficiency causes dopa-responsive dystonia or infantile parkinsonism. Inherited deficiency of DAT or VMAT2 (genes SLC6A3 and SLC18A2) causes infantile parkinsonism–dystonia. Reduced choline acetyltransferase activity has been reported in Alzheimer's disease. Autoantibodies to GAD are associated with stiff-person syndrome. | More synthesis, release, or recycling than normal raises transmitter output. Stimulant drugs that reverse DAT flood synapses with dopamine and cause agitation, psychosis, and stereotyped movements. Too much levodopa (a dopamine precursor) causes dyskinesias. Rare DAT variants that leak dopamine have been reported in autism and ADHD. | Many antidepressants and stimulants act by blocking transporters. That is a drug effect, not a deficiency. A missing transporter can paradoxically raise extracellular transmitter while still disrupting normal signaling. |

### Structural, transport, and myelin proteins

| Protein | Main functions | Effects of too little | Effects of too much | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Kinesin and dynein motor proteins** | Molecular motors that move cargo (mitochondria, vesicles, receptors, and signaling molecules) along microtubules in axons and dendrites, over distances that can reach a meter or more in humans. | Reduced or faulty axonal transport deprives synapses and distant parts of neurons of supplies. Variants in motor-protein genes such as KIF1A and KIF5A cause neurodevelopmental disorders, hereditary spastic paraplegia, and ALS-associated forms of disease. | Some variants in KIF1A and KIF5A make motors abnormally active or remove their auto-inhibition, and have been linked to neurodevelopmental disorders and ALS. Too much motor activity can disturb cargo delivery as well as too little. | Many of these disorders reflect altered function rather than simple absence, so "too little" is an approximation. |
| **Myelin basic protein (MBP) and proteolipid protein (PLP1)** | Hold the layers of the myelin sheath tightly together. MBP and PLP1 are major protein components of central nervous system myelin. | Thin or unstable myelin and slowed conduction. Mutations in PLP1 cause Pelizaeus–Merzbacher disease (delayed motor development, abnormal eye movements, and spasticity). Mice lacking MBP (the "shiverer" mouse) have severe hypomyelination, tremor, and seizures. | Extra copies of PLP1 are the most common cause of Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease, in which too much PLP1 leads to poor myelin formation, delayed development, abnormal eye movements, and spasticity. Excess MBP has not been established as a cause of disease. | PLP1 disease can also arise from too much protein (gene duplications). MBP is among the targets of the immune attack in multiple sclerosis, which is not a simple "too little" state. |

### Energy, barrier, and support proteins

| Protein | Main functions | Effects of too little | Effects of too much | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **GLUT1** (glucose transporter 1; encoded by SLC2A1) | Carries glucose across the blood–brain barrier and into astrocytes. Glucose is the brain's main fuel. | GLUT1 deficiency syndrome: seizures beginning in infancy, developmental delay, movement problems, and low glucose in the cerebrospinal fluid. A ketogenic diet, which supplies ketones as an alternative fuel, is an established treatment. | Excess GLUT1 is not a defined brain syndrome. GLUT1 is increased in many tumors, including gliomas, which supports their high glucose use. | A clear example of a single transporter limiting brain function. Severity varies between people. |
| **Sodium–potassium ATPase** (for example the alpha-3 subunit, encoded by ATP1A3) | A pump that restores the sodium and potassium gradients after signaling. It uses a large share of the brain's energy budget. | Variants that reduce pump function in ATP1A3 cause alternating hemiplegia of childhood and rapid-onset dystonia–parkinsonism. | Too much pump activity is not an established cause of disease. The clinical problems are reduced function or an energy supply that cannot keep up with demand during intense activity. | Different variants cause different syndromes, so the type of change matters as well as the amount. |
| **Aquaporin-4** (AQP4) | A water channel concentrated on astrocyte endfeet around blood vessels. It helps handle water and potassium and has been proposed to take part in fluid clearance (the "glymphatic" system). | When autoantibodies target AQP4 (neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder), astrocytes are damaged, causing inflammation in the optic nerves and spinal cord and sometimes the brain. | After stroke or injury, higher AQP4 activity is thought to worsen astrocyte swelling (cytotoxic edema) in animal models, while deleting it reduces early swelling but slows fluid clearance later. | The glymphatic role of AQP4 in humans is still being debated. |
| **Tight-junction proteins** (claudin-5, occludin, ZO-1) | Seal the gaps between endothelial cells so that the blood–brain barrier restricts what enters brain tissue (Section 2). | A leakier barrier. In mice, reducing claudin-5 increases barrier permeability to small molecules. Changes in barrier proteins have been reported in several disorders. | A tighter barrier protects the brain but also keeps most drugs out, which makes it hard to deliver medicines to the brain. Excess barrier proteins are not a defined disease. | Whether barrier changes cause or result from disease is often unclear. |
| **Apolipoprotein E** (ApoE) | Carries cholesterol and other lipids in the brain, mainly made by astrocytes. In humans, complete absence is rare, and the main issue is which version (isoform) a person carries rather than how much. | "Too much" is poorly defined for this protein. The version matters more than the amount: the ε4 form raises Alzheimer's risk, and approaches to lower ApoE4 have been explored in preclinical work. | The ε4 version raises the risk of Alzheimer's disease but does not determine it. "Too low" is poorly defined for this protein. |

### Development and gene-regulation proteins

| Protein | Main functions | Effects of too little | Effects of too much | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **BDNF** (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) | A growth factor that supports neuron survival, growth, synapse formation, and activity-dependent plasticity. | Reduced synaptic maintenance and plasticity and impaired learning in animals. Lower BDNF levels have been reported in blood from some people with depression, and in Alzheimer's and Huntington's disease. | In animals, excess BDNF signaling can promote seizure development and heighten pain sensitivity in the spinal cord, and in reward circuits it is linked to stronger drug seeking. Human relevance is not established. | These are associations. Blood BDNF does not equal brain BDNF, and the causal role in human conditions is debated. Exercise raises BDNF in animals and some human blood measures (Section 11). |
| **Reelin** | A secreted protein that guides migrating neurons to form the layers of the cerebral cortex and cerebellum during development. | **MeCP2** (methyl-CpG binding protein 2) | Reads chemical marks on DNA and regulates the activity of many genes in neurons, supporting mature neuron function and synaptic plasticity. | Loss-of-function mutations in MECP2 cause Rett syndrome: apparently typical early development followed by regression of speech and hand use, and often seizures and breathing abnormalities. | MECP2 duplication syndrome: intellectual disability, floppy muscle tone in infancy, recurrent respiratory infections, seizures, and autism features, mostly in boys. | **FMRP** (fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein) | Regulates the local production of proteins at synapses in response to activity. | Silencing of the FMR1 gene removes FMRP and causes fragile X syndrome, the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability, often with anxiety and autism-like features. | Too much FMRP is not a defined condition. | **FOXP2** | A transcription factor involved in the development of circuits for speech and language, including cortico-striatal and cerebellar circuits. | Loss of one working copy causes a severe speech and language disorder with difficulty coordinating speech movements. | Excess FOXP2 is not a defined human disorder. In songbirds, increasing as well as reducing FoxP2 in song-learning circuits has been reported to disrupt vocal learning, suggesting dosage matters in both directions. | FOXP2 is not "the language gene." ### Proteins best known through disease

These proteins have normal jobs that are easy to miss because they are most famous for clumping or misbehaving in disease. For each, the questions of "too little" and "too much" matter because they determine whether therapies should replace, preserve, or reduce the protein.

| Protein | Main functions | Effects of too little | Effects of too much | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tau** | Binds and stabilizes microtubules in axons, helping axonal transport, and has additional roles at synapses. | Loss of normal tau function is thought to destabilize microtubules and impair axonal transport. However, deleting tau in mice produces a surprisingly mild phenotype. Disease is believed to involve both loss of normal function and a toxic gain, in which misfolded, over-phosphorylated tau forms tangles. | Abnormally phosphorylated, misfolded tau clumps into neurofibrillary tangles and is linked to neuron loss. MAPT mutations cause some frontotemporal dementias, and abnormal tau appears to spread between connected regions as disease progresses. | Tangles define Alzheimer's disease and other tauopathies. Whether any single mechanism is primary is not settled. |
| **Amyloid precursor protein** (APP) | A membrane protein implicated in neurite growth, synapse formation and maintenance, and repair after injury. It is cut into fragments, including amyloid-beta. | In mice, lacking APP leads to smaller brain and body size, impaired plasticity, and learning deficits, but related proteins partly compensate. Effects of low APP in humans are unclear. | Extra copies of the APP gene cause early-onset Alzheimer's disease. People with Down syndrome have three copies and develop Alzheimer's-type changes in the brain, usually by middle age. Excess amyloid-beta builds into plaques. | Amyloid-beta is a fragment, not the whole protein. Anti-amyloid therapies (Section 7) target aggregated amyloid-beta rather than normal APP. |
| **Alpha-synuclein** | An abundant presynaptic protein that helps organize synaptic vesicles and assemble the release machinery. | Its normal function is incompletely understood. Mice lacking it show only mild changes in dopamine release. In Parkinson's disease and Lewy body disease, it clumps into aggregates, probably through a toxic gain, with some contribution from loss of normal function. | Duplications and triplications of the SNCA gene cause familial Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, and the effect is dose-dependent: triplication causes earlier and more severe disease. | Extra copies of the gene (duplications and triplications) cause familial Parkinson's disease, so too much is clearly harmful. |
| **Huntingtin** | A large scaffold protein involved in vesicle and BDNF transport and gene regulation. | Complete loss is lethal in early development in mice. Huntington's disease is caused mainly by a toxic gain from an expanded repeat, but reduced normal huntingtin may contribute. | Huntington's disease arises from an expanded CAG repeat (generally 40 or more repeats) that makes a toxic mutant protein: involuntary movements (chorea), cognitive decline, and psychiatric symptoms. The problem is the abnormal protein, not too much normal huntingtin. | Matters for therapies that aim to lower huntingtin: lowering too much could remove its normal functions. |
| **TDP-43** | An RNA-binding protein, mostly in the nucleus, that regulates how RNA is spliced and processed. | In ALS and frontotemporal dementia, TDP-43 is depleted from nuclei and clumps in the cytoplasm. Loss of nuclear TDP-43 causes RNA-splicing errors that reduce proteins needed for axon maintenance, such as stathmin-2. | Overexpression is toxic to neurons in animal models, and cytoplasmic aggregates of TDP-43 are a hallmark of ALS and frontotemporal dementia. TARDBP mutations cause some inherited ALS. | Illustrates a disease with both loss of function and toxic gain. |

### What "too low" can and cannot mean for proteins

- **"Too little" has several causes.** A protein can be reduced by a gene variant, lower gene expression, autoantibodies, mislocalization, faster breakdown, or loss of the cells that make it. Each cause has different implications for treatment.
- **Loss and gain often coexist.** Several proteins here (tau, alpha-synuclein, TDP-43, and huntingtin) are linked to disease through both losing normal function and gaining a toxic form. Both too much and too little of a protein, as with MeCP2 and PLP1, can cause disease.
- **Mouse results do not always carry over.** Many "too little" effects described here come from knockout mice. Humans can differ in how much other proteins compensate.
- **Markers are not functions.** Some brain proteins, such as phosphorylated tau, amyloid-beta, neurofilament light, and GFAP, are measured in cerebrospinal fluid or blood as biomarkers in clinical and research settings. A biomarker level reports on a disease process and is not the same as the protein's function.
- **Variants are not destiny.** A gene variant can raise or lower risk without determining the outcome, and the same variant can cause different outcomes in different people. Genetic testing and interpretation belong with qualified clinicians and genetic counselors.
- **No self-diagnosis.** Nothing here allows a person to infer the state of any particular protein from symptoms, scores, or feelings.

### What "too high" can and cannot mean for proteins

- **"Too much" has several causes.** A protein can be raised by extra gene copies, gain-of-function variants that make it more active, overexpression, failure of clearance, misfolding and aggregation, abnormal activation, or loss of an inhibitory partner. Each cause has different implications for treatment.
- **Amount, activity, and form are different.** More protein, more active protein, and abnormal protein are separate problems. In Huntington's disease the issue is a toxic expanded form, not an excess of normal huntingtin.
- **Dosage-sensitive genes.** Some genes cause disease in both directions: loss of MECP2 causes Rett syndrome and extra copies cause MECP2 duplication syndrome, and the same pattern appears for PLP1, SCN2A, and FOXG1. For others, such as APP (as in Down syndrome) and SNCA, extra copies are the problem.
- **Lowering strategies have limits.** Reducing a toxic or overabundant protein, for example with antisense drugs, can work (an antisense drug is approved for one inherited form of ALS). But lowering too much can remove normal function, and a large trial of a huntingtin-lowering drug was stopped in 2021 after an unfavorable benefit–risk balance at the dose tested.
- **Overexpression experiments can mislead.** Animals engineered to overproduce a protein can show effects that reflect artificial levels or locations, so findings need confirmation.
- **Raised biomarkers are not necessarily causes.** Higher blood or cerebrospinal fluid levels of proteins such as neurofilament light, GFAP, and phosphorylated tau mostly report injury or a disease process and are not by themselves evidence that the protein is the cause.
- **Variants are not destiny.** Extra copies and gain-of-function variants raise risk or cause disease with variable severity, and interpretation belongs with qualified clinicians and genetic counselors.
- **No self-diagnosis.** Nothing here lets a person infer how much of any protein they have from symptoms, scores, or feelings.

**Consciousness relevance:** several proteins in this section sit at the boundary between molecules and conscious level.
- **Receptors as anesthetic targets.** GABA-A receptors and NMDA receptors are major targets of anesthetic and sedative drugs, so their function bears directly on the loss and recovery of responsiveness (Section 3).
- **Receptor loss and awareness.** Autoimmune removal of NMDA receptors can alter thinking, behavior, and awareness, and the problem can reverse with treatment. This shows that molecular changes can change consciousness without destroying neurons.
- **Energy and excitability.** Proteins such as GLUT1, the sodium–potassium pump, and Nav1.1 set the energy supply and excitability that make normal activity possible, and their failure causes seizures and altered awareness.
- **No protein is a consciousness meter.** None of these molecules is established as the generator of experience, and measuring one does not tell us whether a person is conscious.

## 2C. Major genes of the human genome and the brain

Section 2B described proteins; this section steps one level up to the genes that encode them and the regulatory DNA that controls when and where they are used. The human genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, and a large majority are active somewhere in the brain. Genes do not "contain" behavior. They set up the molecules, cell types, timing, and wiring rules from which brains develop, and they interact throughout life with biology, experience, and environment. This section covers genes that have strong, well-documented effects on how the brain forms and grows, genes that shape circuits and behavior, genes in aging and degeneration, and the common-variant and rare-variant findings in psychiatric genetics. It is a selected guide, not a catalog: hundreds of genes are now linked to neurodevelopmental conditions alone.

**How to read the tables**
- **What it does** summarizes the gene's product and its role in the brain.
- **Effects of variants** describes what is reported when the gene is lost, reduced, extra, or altered. Many entries concern rare, single-gene conditions with large effects. Where a gene is known mainly for small statistical associations, the caveats column says so.
- **Dosage matters.** Some genes cause disease when one copy is lost (haploinsufficiency), some when an extra copy is present, and some in both directions. The entries flag this where it is known, connecting to the "too little" and "too much" columns in Section 2B.
- These are educational summaries. A gene name in a table does not allow anyone to infer a person's abilities, personality, or risk. Genetic testing and interpretation belong with qualified clinicians and genetic counselors.

### Genes and the timeline of brain development

Genes are used in waves, and the timing explains why variants in different genes affect different stages.

| Stage (approximate) | What is happening | Example genes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Patterning and neural tube closure** (first weeks of gestation) | The embryo sets up front–back and top–bottom axes, divides the forebrain into two hemispheres, and forms regional identities. | SHH, PAX6, FOXG1 |
| **Neurogenesis** (mainly weeks 6 to 24) | Progenitor cells divide and produce neurons. How many divisions occur largely determines cortical size. | ASPM, WDR62, PTEN, mTOR-pathway genes |
| **Migration and layering** (overlapping with neurogenesis) | New neurons travel to their final positions and form cortical layers. | LIS1, DCX, RELN, ARX |
| **Synapse formation, myelination, and pruning** (late gestation through adolescence and early adulthood) | Synapses are overproduced, strengthened by activity, and then pruned. Myelination continues into the third decade. | SHANK3, SYNGAP1, NRXN1, PLP1, C4 |
| **Maintenance and aging** (adulthood onward) | Cells and circuits must be maintained, repaired, and cleared of waste. | APOE, TREM2, GBA1, MAPT |

### Patterning, migration, and layering

| Gene (protein) | What it does | Effects of variants | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **SHH** (sonic hedgehog) | A signaling molecule that patterns the ventral forebrain and midline in the early embryo. Later, it drives growth of cerebellar progenitor cells. | Loss of function can cause holoprosencephaly, in which the forebrain fails to divide into two hemispheres, usually with facial midline differences and developmental delay. | Holoprosencephaly has several genetic and environmental causes. | **PAX6** | A transcription factor needed for eye and forebrain development. | One non-working copy causes aniridia (absence or underdevelopment of the iris) and in some people changes in brain structures such as the corpus callosum, sometimes with cognitive or neurodevelopmental differences. | Neurological effects vary widely between people. |
| **FOXG1** | A transcription factor needed for growth of the cerebral hemispheres (telencephalon). | One non-working copy causes FOXG1 syndrome: reduced head growth after birth, severe developmental delay, absent or very limited speech, movement disorder, and seizures. | **LIS1** (PAFAH1B1) | Works with microtubule motor proteins to move neurons to their destinations. | Loss of one copy causes classic lissencephaly ("smooth brain"), with severe intellectual disability and epilepsy. | **DCX** (doublecortin; on the X chromosome) | A microtubule-associated protein required for neuronal migration. | In males, variants cause lissencephaly. | **ARX** (on the X chromosome) | A transcription factor needed for forebrain development and the migration of inhibitory interneurons. | Variants cause a spectrum from X-linked lissencephaly with abnormal genitalia to infantile spasms and intellectual disability without major structural change. | Different variants in the same gene can produce very different severities. |
| **RELN** (reelin) | A guidance protein for neuronal migration (see Section 2B). | Two non-working copies cause lissencephaly with cerebellar hypoplasia. One non-working copy can cause a familial form of temporal lobe epilepsy. | Included here because it is a core migration gene. |

### Brain size and growth

| Gene (protein) | What it does | Effects of variants | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ASPM, WDR62, CDK5RAP2, MCPH1, CENPJ** (primary microcephaly genes) | Proteins at the centrosome and spindle that control how neural progenitor cells divide. | Two non-working copies cause autosomal recessive primary microcephaly: a markedly small head at birth, a small cerebral cortex with largely preserved layering, and intellectual disability. | **TSC1 and TSC2** (hamartin and tuberin) | Restrain mTOR signaling. | Loss of function causes tuberous sclerosis complex: cortical tubers, seizures (including infantile spasms), intellectual disability, autism, and benign brain growths. | Medicines that inhibit mTOR are approved for some manifestations, an example of treatment aimed at the pathway rather than the symptom. |
| **DEPDC5, MTOR, and related mTOR-pathway genes** | Regulate mTOR signaling in developing cortex. | Variants, often arising in only a subset of brain cells (somatic mosaicism), cause focal cortical dysplasia, hemimegalencephaly (one enlarged hemisphere), and drug-resistant epilepsy. | Shows that a variant need not be present in every cell to alter circuit function. |
| **Human-lineage genes** (for example NOTCH2NL, ARHGAP11B, SRGAP2C) | Recently duplicated genes found in humans. | **Human accelerated regions** (for example HAR1) | Short stretches of non-coding DNA that are conserved in other species but changed rapidly in humans. ### Larger genomic changes with effects on the brain

Some conditions arise from losing or gaining a block of DNA that contains many genes rather than a single gene.

| Change | Brain and behavior effects | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trisomy 21** (Down syndrome; an extra copy of chromosome 21) | Intellectual disability of variable degree, floppy muscle tone, and a high likelihood of Alzheimer's-type brain changes by middle age (the APP gene is on chromosome 21). | Roughly one in 700 births. Skills and needs vary widely between individuals. |
| **22q11.2 deletion** | Learning difficulties, anxiety, ADHD, and a substantially increased risk of psychotic disorders in adulthood (roughly one in four in clinical samples). | One of the strongest known single genetic risk factors for schizophrenia, though most people with the deletion do not develop psychosis. | **16p11.2 deletion and duplication** | The deletion is associated with a larger head, autism, intellectual disability, and obesity. The duplication is associated with a smaller head, autism, schizophrenia risk, and low body weight. | A mirror-image pattern: opposite changes in copy number shift head size and body weight in opposite directions. |
| **Williams syndrome** (7q11.23 deletion) | Intellectual disability or learning difficulties, strong sociability and anxiety, relative strengths in language and music, and weaker visuospatial skills. | The reciprocal duplication is associated with speech delay and social anxiety, an example of opposite dosage changes producing different behavioral profiles. |
| **Prader-Willi syndrome** (loss of the paternal 15q11-q13 region) | Floppy muscle tone in infancy, intense hunger and difficulty feeling full (hypothalamic involvement), intellectual disability, and compulsive behaviors. | Shares its chromosomal region with Angelman syndrome. Which parent the missing copy came from determines the condition. |

### Neurotransmitter, circadian, and behavior-related genes

This group is where careful reading matters most. A few variants have large effects, but many findings celebrated in the 2000s were small, and several did not hold up when tested in much larger samples.

| Gene (protein) | What it does | What has been reported | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **COMT** (catechol-O-methyltransferase) | Breaks down dopamine, especially in prefrontal cortex. | The Val158Met variant changes enzyme activity. Not a basis for predicting a person's abilities or anxiety. |

It is a cautionary example for the whole field. |

### From genes to behavior: what genetics can and cannot say

- **Most traits are polygenic.** Intelligence, personality, educational attainment, and most psychiatric conditions are influenced by thousands of common variants of very small individual effect, plus rare variants and environment. Large genome-wide studies now identify hundreds of loci for several of these traits.
- **Single-gene stories are the exception.** The syndromes above are real and informative, but they account for a minority of people with neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions. "The gene for" intelligence, aggression, or happiness does not exist.
- **Heritability is not determinism.** Twin and family studies show that many traits are partly heritable, but heritability describes variation within a particular population and environment. It does not mean a trait is fixed or cannot be influenced.
- **Penetrance and expressivity vary.** The same variant can cause severe disease in one person and mild or no symptoms in another, depending on other genes, chance, and environment.
- **Pleiotropy is common.** One gene often influences several traits, and one condition can arise from many genes. CACNA1C, SCN2A, and the 22q11.2 deletion each affect several outcomes.
- **Gene and environment interact.** Nutrition, infection, stress, sleep, toxins, and experience change which genes are active (epigenetics) and how circuits are shaped. Early candidate-gene-by-environment findings such as 5-HTTLPR show how easily these claims can fail.
- **Polygenic scores predict little for any one person.** They can shift statistical risk across a population but are poor at predicting an individual's outcome, and they perform worse in people whose ancestry differs from the groups used to build them, so they risk widening health inequalities.
- **Dosage matters in both directions.** Compare SCN1A, FOXG1, and SYNGAP1 (loss of one copy causes disease) with MECP2, PLP1, APP, and SNCA (extra copies cause disease).
- **Testing and treatment are advancing.** Exome or genome sequencing finds a genetic diagnosis in roughly one-third or more of children evaluated for unexplained developmental delay or intellectual disability. A diagnosis can end a long search, guide seizure treatment, and inform family planning. Gene-targeted treatments now exist for some conditions, including antisense and gene therapies for spinal muscular atrophy and an antisense drug for SOD1-related ALS, and an individually designed antisense drug was made for one child with a form of Batten disease. These remain exceptions, and each approach carries risks and costs.
- **Privacy and fairness matter.** Genetic information is sensitive. In the United States, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act protects against misuse in health insurance and employment, but not in life, disability, or long-term care insurance. Decisions about testing should involve genetic counseling.

**Consciousness relevance:** genes build the machinery that supports conscious level and content, but none is a "consciousness gene."
- **Development and wiring.** Genes that control the thalamocortical connections, cortical layers, and inhibitory interneurons shape the circuits that the theories in Section 4 discuss, and defects in them can alter awareness (for example, severe epilepsy syndromes).
- **Sleep and arousal.** Clock and orexin-related genes influence timing and stability of wakefulness.
- **Anesthesia sensitivity.** Genes for GABA-A and NMDA receptor subunits help determine how people respond to sedatives and anesthetics.
- **No genetic test reveals whether a person is conscious.** Genetic findings do not decide between the competing theories in Section 4.

Sources: [Siletti et al., *Science* (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37824663/) for cell types and gene expression across the adult human brain (see Section 2); the NCBI [GeneReviews](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1116/) collection for the clinical genetics of the syndromes above; and Border et al., *American Journal of Psychiatry* (2019), for the failure of depression candidate-gene findings in large samples.

## 3. How the brain works: a multilevel view

A useful model links several levels instead of choosing only one:

1. **Molecules and genes:** receptors, ion channels, neurotransmitter synthesis, gene expression, and cellular energy metabolism constrain what cells can do.
2. **Cells:** neurons and glia have diverse types and properties. Cell identity depends on molecular profile, anatomy, connections, and physiology.
3. **Synapses and local circuits:** groups of cells transform signals through excitation, inhibition, recurrent loops, and plasticity.
4. **Large-scale networks:** regions communicate through white matter and dynamic functional coupling. Network organization changes with task and brain state.
5. **Cognition and behavior:** perception, planning, language, memory, emotion, and action emerge from coordinated biological processes.
6. **Body and environment:** sensory input, movement, social context, hormones, sleep, learning, and culture shape brain function over time.

### Perception is inference, not a camera feed

Sensory systems transform physical energy into neural signals. The brain combines these signals with context, attention, prior learning, and expectations to construct useful interpretations. This often produces stable, accurate perception, but it also creates illusions and systematic errors.

**Predictive processing** is a family of models proposing that the brain continually generates predictions and updates them using prediction errors. It is influential and useful, but the claim that one predictive principle explains all brain function is not settled. Competing and complementary accounts emphasize feedforward feature processing, recurrent processing, attention, active sensing, and task-specific learning.

**Consciousness relevance:** a percept is not necessarily conscious just because sensory information has been processed. Some processing occurs without reportable awareness. Researchers debate which additional operations—recurrent interactions, global availability, higher-order representation, or other features—are required for conscious access.

### Attention, working memory, and decision-making

- **Attention** prioritizes information or tasks; it can be driven by goals or by salient events.
- **Working memory** temporarily maintains and manipulates information. It relies on distributed circuits and may involve persistent activity, short-term synaptic changes, or dynamic activity patterns depending on the task.
- **Decision-making** combines evidence, goals, value, uncertainty, and constraints. It is not located in one "decision center."
- **Executive control** helps maintain goals, suppress some responses, switch tasks, and monitor errors. These abilities are limited and depend on fatigue, stress, development, and context.

**Consciousness relevance:** attention and consciousness are related but not identical. Something can influence behavior without being consciously noticed, and people can sometimes report experience without strong focused attention. How much attention is necessary for different forms of consciousness remains disputed.

### Learning, memory, and identity

Memory is not one storage system. Working memory, episodic memory, semantic knowledge, skills, habits, emotional learning, and conditioning depend on partially distinct but interacting circuits. Memories are reconstructive: retrieval can alter how an experience is later remembered. Sleep contributes to memory processing, though the mechanisms and benefits differ across memory types.

A person's identity depends on memory, bodily continuity, values, social relationships, habits, and ongoing brain activity. No single memory store or brain location fully contains a person's identity.

**Consciousness relevance:** the present moment can be conscious without an accurate autobiographical memory of it. Conversely, memory reports can be distorted. This complicates attempts to infer past conscious experience from later testimony alone.

### Emotion, motivation, and the body

Emotion includes changes in attention, physiology, action readiness, interpretation, and subjective feeling. The traditional idea that each emotion has one dedicated brain location is too simple. Threat, reward, sadness, anger, and social connection involve overlapping networks and bodily processes.

Interoception—the sensing and interpretation of internal bodily conditions—contributes to feelings of hunger, breathlessness, pain, arousal, and emotion. The body can alter what a situation feels like and how it is judged.

**Consciousness relevance:** a scientific explanation of emotion must account for both measurable processes and felt experience. Brain imaging can identify correlates of emotional states but cannot, by itself, read a person's full subjective experience.

### Sleep and anesthesia

Sleep is an active biological state, not merely the brain "turning off." Non-REM and REM sleep have distinct patterns; sleep supports health, learning, emotional regulation, and metabolic functions. Dreams are common in some sleep states, but vivid experience can occur in others too.

General anesthesia changes brain dynamics and responsiveness through drugs acting on multiple molecular targets and circuits. Different anesthetics can produce unconsciousness through partly different mechanisms. Loss of responsiveness is not always identical to absence of all experience, and reports after emergence are imperfect evidence about what occurred during anesthesia.

**Consciousness relevance:** sleep, anesthesia, coma, and disorders of consciousness offer powerful—but ethically and methodologically challenging—ways to study the conditions associated with conscious experience.

## 4. Consciousness: what is known and what remains open

### Distinguish the questions

- **Wakefulness/arousal:** Is the brain in a state that can support responsiveness?
- **Awareness:** Is there experience or awareness of the environment or self?
- **Conscious content:** What is being experienced—color, pain, a sound, a thought?
- **Access consciousness:** Is information available for flexible reasoning, decision-making, and report?
- **Self-consciousness:** Does experience include a representation of oneself, one's body, or one's thoughts?
- **Phenomenal consciousness:** What does an experience feel like from the inside?

These questions overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A patient may be awake but have severely impaired awareness; a person may have experience without being able to communicate it; and reportability can fail even when some processing remains.

### Broad areas of agreement

1. Conscious states are systematically associated with brain states and are altered by lesions, seizures, anesthesia, sleep, psychoactive substances, and stimulation.
2. Conscious contents are associated with activity in distributed systems, including sensory and association cortex, with recurrent interactions and state-dependent coordination.
3. No single brain scan or behavioral test perfectly determines whether an uncommunicative person is conscious.
4. Neural correlates are not automatically a complete explanation. Finding what reliably accompanies an experience does not by itself explain why that activity has a subjective quality.
5. Strong tests require clear definitions, preregistered predictions, multiple measurement methods, and efforts to distinguish conscious experience from attention, memory, task demands, and reporting.

### Major competing theories

| Theory | Core proposal | What it helps explain | Main challenges |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT)** | Information becomes conscious when it is amplified and made broadly available to multiple systems ("global broadcasting" or "ignition"). | Conscious access, report, flexible use of information, and some late neural responses. | Some predicted all-or-none ignition and strong prefrontal signatures have not been consistently observed; report and task demands can confound results. |
| **Integrated Information Theory (IIT)** | Consciousness corresponds to a system's intrinsic cause–effect structure and integrated information; some versions predict a posterior cortical "hot zone." | Attempts to explain unity and intrinsic structure of experience. | Its central quantities are difficult to measure in real brains; some predictions are controversial or challenged by recent tests; implications can extend to systems many consider implausible. |
| **Recurrent Processing Theory (RPT)** | Local recurrent processing in sensory cortex may be sufficient for perceptual experience, while broader processing may be needed for report and reasoning. | Distinguishes perceptual experience from later decision/report processes. | Difficult to isolate recurrence from attention, feedback, and other correlated processes in humans. |
| **Higher-Order Theories (HOT)** | A mental state is conscious when represented by a suitable higher-order state about that state. | Metacognition and the sense of being aware of a mental state. | Debate about whether higher-order representation is necessary, and how to test it without relying on verbal report. |
| **Predictive-processing / predictive-coding accounts** | Perception and cognition involve hierarchical prediction and error correction. Some versions connect consciousness to precision-weighted inference. | Perception, illusion, expectation, and the role of prior beliefs. | A broad framework rather than one uniquely specified consciousness theory; flexible versions can be hard to falsify. |
| **Attention schema theory** | The brain constructs a simplified model of its own attention, which helps explain why it attributes awareness to itself and others. | Self-report and social judgments about awareness. | The relationship between a model of attention and subjective experience itself remains contested. |
| **Enactive / embodied approaches** | Consciousness and cognition depend on active organism–environment interaction, bodily regulation, and sensorimotor engagement. | Embodiment, action, and the context-dependence of experience. | Some versions are broad philosophical frameworks and do not yet produce decisive, unique neural predictions. |
| **Biological naturalism and other physicalist views** | Consciousness is a natural phenomenon dependent on biological processes, though the exact explanatory bridge is unresolved. | Takes brain dependence seriously without committing to one circuit-level mechanism. | Can be more of a philosophical position than a testable, detailed mechanism. |
| **Quantum-based proposals** (for example Orchestrated Objective Reduction, Orch OR) | Consciousness depends on quantum processes in the brain, such as coherence in neuronal microtubules ending in an objective collapse. | Attempts to link experience to fundamental physics, and some indirect findings about microtubules and anesthesia. | No direct evidence of functionally relevant quantum coherence in living brains. Calculated decoherence times are very short, and many researchers favor classical explanations (Section 4A). |
| **Dualist or non-physical views** | Mind or experience is not wholly reducible to physical processes. | Addresses intuitions about subjective experience and the explanatory gap. | Difficult to derive distinctive, testable predictions that outperform brain-based explanations. |

These theories are not all at the same level: some make detailed neural predictions, while others are broader philosophical or cognitive frameworks. Quantum-based proposals, which differ from the others in the mechanism they invoke, are examined in Section 4A.

### A major recent test: GNWT versus IIT

A 2025 *Nature* study organized an adversarial collaboration between proponents of GNWT and IIT. Researchers studied 256 participants across fMRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial EEG. Results supported some predictions of each theory but challenged key claims of both. In particular, the expected sustained posterior synchronization for IIT was not found, while some strong GNWT predictions about late "ignition" and prefrontal representation were also challenged. The authors argued for more rigorous, quantitative, theory-driven tests rather than declaring a winner. [Cogitate Consortium et al., *Nature* (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1).

**Interpretation:** the study is a meaningful advance, not proof that consciousness has been solved or that either theory is entirely false. It tested specific predictions in particular tasks and modalities; the theories' advocates may revise biological implementations, and other theories remain in play.

### Consciousness in medicine

Clinical assessment distinguishes level of arousal from evidence of awareness. Disorders such as coma, unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, and minimally conscious state require careful assessment over time. Some patients who cannot follow commands may show brain responses consistent with covert command-following on specialized tests. Such findings matter for prognosis and communication, but no one method is infallible.

Consciousness is also central to pain, psychiatric care, anesthesia, end-of-life decisions, and animal welfare. Ethical practice requires humility: absence of outward response is not definitive proof of absence of experience.

## 4A. Quantum physics and the brain: a deep dive

Section 4 introduced the major theories of consciousness. This section examines a different and more contested question: does quantum physics do anything for the brain beyond the obvious, and can it help explain behavior or consciousness? The honest answer has three layers. Quantum mechanics is the foundation of all chemistry, so it underlies every brain process in a trivial sense. Whether specific quantum effects (coherence, tunneling, entanglement) play a functional role in neurons is an open and mostly speculative research question. And whether quantum physics is needed to explain consciousness is a minority position that has not been demonstrated. This section keeps those layers separate, because most confusion in popular discussion comes from sliding between them.

**How to read this section**
- **"Quantum" does not mean "mysterious" or "conscious."** It names a set of physical rules that govern atoms, molecules, and light.
- **Three questions, three different evidence standards.** (1) Does the brain depend on quantum mechanics? Yes, as all matter does. (2) Do non-trivial quantum effects change how neurons compute? Unknown. (3) Is consciousness a quantum phenomenon? Unproven and contested.
- **Evidence is graded.** Entries below are marked as established, supported but debated, or speculative.
- This is an educational overview of a fast-moving and contested area, not a conclusion. As elsewhere in this document, correlations and plausibility arguments are not proofs.

### 4A.1 Quantum physics in plain language

| Concept | What it means | Why it matters for the brain debate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quantization** | Energy, light, and electron states come in discrete levels. | Explains why atoms are stable and why molecules have specific shapes and bonds. This underlies protein structure, drug binding, and every chemical reaction in neurons. |
| **Superposition** | Until measured or disturbed, a quantum system can be described as having several possible states at once. | The key resource that proposed quantum computation in the brain would need. |
| **Entanglement** | Two quantum systems can share a joint state so that measuring one gives information about the other, however far apart. | Proposed (but not shown) as a way for distant brain parts to be correlated. |
| **Tunneling** | A particle can pass through an energy barrier that classical physics says it cannot cross. | Well documented in enzyme reactions and in many chemical processes. |
| **Spin** | An intrinsic property of particles that behaves like a tiny magnet. Nuclear spins interact weakly with their surroundings. | Basis of MRI, and of proposals that nuclear spins could preserve quantum states. |
| **Coherence and decoherence** | Coherence is a stable phase relationship between quantum states. Decoherence is its loss through interaction with the environment. | The central obstacle: a warm, wet, crowded brain is thought to destroy coherence very quickly. |
| **The measurement problem** | Quantum theory predicts probabilities, and physicists disagree about what happens when a measurement yields a single outcome. | Source of proposals that consciousness is linked to "collapse." These are interpretations, and no experiment has established them. |

### 4A.2 Three layers of the question

| Layer | Claim | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Quantum mechanics underlies the brain's chemistry and physics** | Atoms, bonds, ion channel structures, protein folding, light absorption in the retina, drug binding, and MRI signals all follow quantum rules. | **Established.** Brains are made of matter. Neuroscientists use quantum chemistry to model molecules, but this does not mean neurons compute with quantum states. |
| **2. Specific quantum effects play a functional role in neurons** | Tunneling, spin dynamics, or coherence contribute to signaling, sensing, or computation in ways that classical descriptions miss. | **Open.** Some quantum effects are established in other biological systems (below). Direct evidence in neurons is limited, disputed, or absent. |
| **3. Quantum processes are needed to explain consciousness or free will** | Consciousness arises from, or requires, quantum computation or a quantum collapse process in the brain. | **Speculative and contested.** Most researchers favor classical explanations. A few indirect experimental findings are consistent with some versions but do not demonstrate them. |

### 4A.3 What is already established in quantum biology

Quantum effects are not confined to physics laboratories. Several biological processes show them, which is why the brain question is a legitimate scientific one and not a fringe notion.

| Process | Quantum element | Status and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Enzyme catalysis** | Hydrogen tunneling speeds many reactions. | Well supported. It is expected to operate in brain enzymes too, but it is chemistry in neurons rather than a distinct quantum "brain function." |
| **Photosynthesis** | Early experiments reported long-lived coherent energy transfer in light-harvesting complexes. | Supported but debated. Later work questioned what the coherence contributes, and 2025 simulations argue that quantum effects persist in primary photosynthesis. The role of coherence in efficiency is still being worked out. |
| **Bird magnetic navigation** | Spin-dependent chemical reactions (the radical-pair mechanism) in light-sensitive cryptochrome proteins may let birds sense the direction of Earth's magnetic field. | Supported by behavioral and laboratory evidence, but not fully proven. Humans have cryptochromes, and a 2019 EEG study reported brain responses to rotated Earth-strength magnetic fields, but it needs independent replication. Human magnetoreception is not established. |
| **Single-photon vision** | Rod cells in the retina respond to individual photons, a quantum event. | A 2016 experiment reported that people can detect single photons at better than chance in a sensitive task. The first step in vision is quantum, though the rest of visual processing is classical neural computation. |
| **Olfaction** | A proposal that smell receptors detect molecular vibrations through electron tunneling. | Disputed. Experiments have not supported it, and most researchers favor shape-based receptor binding. |

### 4A.4 Candidate quantum mechanisms in the brain

| Proposal | The idea | Evidence for | Main objections | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tunneling in ion channels and synaptic release** | Ions or protons may tunnel through channel pores or vesicle-release barriers. | Quantum chemistry is used to model channels, and the ion selectivity filter has been studied with quantum-level methods. | Little experimental evidence that tunneling or coherence shapes channel behavior beyond what chemistry already predicts. A 1990s proposal that quantum tunneling controls vesicle release remains unsupported. | Speculative |
| **Radical pairs and spin chemistry** | Magnetic-field-sensitive reactions in proteins such as cryptochrome could influence neural signaling. | The mechanism is credible in birds. Some animal and human studies report weak magnetic-field effects on behavior or brain activity. | Human effects are small, inconsistent, and unreplicated. Physical plausibility at Earth-strength fields is debated. | Open |
| **Nuclear spins and Posner molecules** (Matthew Fisher, 2015) | Phosphorus nuclear spins in calcium phosphate clusters might hold quantum states for hours, because nuclear spins couple weakly to their environment. Entangled clusters could correlate neurons. | Isotope experiments hint at spin-related effects: lithium isotopes with different nuclear spin differed in effects on rat behavior, and xenon isotopes with different nuclear spin differed in anesthetic potency in mice. | No direct observation of long-lived entanglement in living brains. The isotope findings have conventional alternative explanations and need replication. | Speculative |
| **Microtubule quantum coherence** (Orch OR, Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff) | Quantum vibrations in microtubules in neurons are "orchestrated" and then undergo an objective collapse tied to spacetime, producing moments of conscious experience. | A 2024 rat study found a microtubule-stabilizing drug delayed the loss of consciousness under an anesthetic gas. A 2024 laboratory study reported superradiance (a collective quantum light effect) in networks of tryptophan molecules found in microtubule-like structures. | Microtubules matter for anesthesia and neuron structure, but whether the effect is quantum is a further step. A gravity-based collapse mechanism has been tested and the simplest parameter-free version was not supported. Direct measurement in living neurons is lacking. | Contested, minority view |
| **Quantum cognition** | Mathematical tools from quantum theory model human judgments, such as order effects in surveys and the conjunction fallacy. | The models fit several behavioral data sets well. | It makes no claim that the brain is physically quantum. Success of a formalism is not evidence of a quantum mechanism. | A modeling approach, not a claim about brain physics |
| **Biophotons and electromagnetic fields** | Ultra-weak light emitted by neurons, or the brain's own electromagnetic field, might carry information or interact with single cells. | Neurons emit very weak photons, and models propose myelinated axons as light guides. | Whether this signal carries information is unknown, and conventional electrical and chemical signaling already explains neural communication. | Speculative |
| **Claims of "entangled" brain signals in MRI** | A 2022 report suggested MRI signals might indicate entanglement-mediated processes related to consciousness. | A single research group reported it. | Extraordinary claim, needs independent replication and ruling out ordinary physiological and instrumental explanations. | Unreplicated |

### 4A.5 The decoherence objection, step by step

The strongest argument against quantum computation in the brain is the "warm, wet, and noisy" problem. It is worth understanding in detail, because the debate is sharper than the slogan.

1. **The claim.** In 2000, physicist Max Tegmark calculated that quantum superpositions of the kind needed for Orch OR would decay in about 10⁻¹³ seconds in brain tissue, far shorter than the roughly millisecond timescale of neural firing. He concluded that neural processes are classical by a large margin.
2. **The reply.** Hameroff and colleagues argued that Tegmark modeled a different system than the one they proposed, and with corrected parameters they obtained decoherence times of roughly 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ seconds, much longer but still shorter than most neural events.
3. **What is agreed.** Even the corrected figure is brief compared with the timescales of firing, synaptic transmission, and rhythms such as gamma oscillations (tens of milliseconds). Proposals must explain how a very short-lived quantum state could influence slower classical dynamics.
4. **Escape routes proposed.** These include shielding inside protein cavities, energy supplied by living metabolism, ordered water, and nuclear spins (Fisher), which are predicted to be far more stable than electron states.
5. **What is missing.** As far as this review could determine, no experiment has directly measured quantum coherence times in living neural tissue, so the calculations remain theoretical. Laboratory findings such as tryptophan superradiance support the idea that biology can sometimes sustain collective quantum effects in isolated structures, but they are not demonstrations of quantum processing in a working brain.

| Timescale | Value (approximate) | Source of the number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tegmark's decoherence estimate for microtubule superpositions | 10⁻¹³ seconds | Theoretical calculation |
| Corrected estimate from Hameroff and colleagues | 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ seconds | Theoretical calculation |
| Neural action potential | about 10⁻³ seconds | Direct measurement |
| Gamma rhythm cycle (40 Hz) | about 0.025 seconds | Direct measurement |
| Fisher's predicted nuclear-spin coherence in Posner molecules | hours to a day | Theoretical prediction, not directly measured in a living brain |

### 4A.6 What quantum physics can currently explain about brain behavior

| Behavior or function | Is a quantum explanation needed? | Current understanding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Basic neuron signaling** (action potentials, synaptic transmission) | No | Explained by classical electrochemistry, though quantum chemistry underlies the molecules involved. |
| **Vision** | Partly, at the first step | Photon absorption by retinal is quantum. Everything after is classical neural processing. |
| **Smell** | Unclear | Shape-based receptor models are favored. The vibration theory is disputed. |
| **Learning and memory** | Not required | Plasticity is explained by receptors, kinases, and gene expression (Sections 2B and 3). No quantum mechanism is established. |
| **Decision-making and judgment** | Not required | Quantum-like probability models can describe the statistics of choices, but this does not imply a quantum brain. |
| **Anesthesia** | Open | Anesthetics act on several targets including GABA-A and NMDA receptors. Microtubule involvement has been proposed, and experiments show microtubule drugs can alter anesthetic onset in rats, but classical explanations remain possible. |
| **Sleep and circadian timing** | No | Explained by molecular clocks, neuromodulators, and circuits. Radical-pair chemistry in cryptochrome is a suggested minor link to magnetic sensitivity only. |
| **Creativity and insight** | No evidence | Classical neural computation explains much, though not everything, about creative thinking. |
| **Free will** | Not resolved | Quantum randomness would not by itself provide "free" choice. Randomness is not the same as agency, and this question is philosophical as well as scientific. |
| **Consciousness** | Open and contested | No accepted account of experience exists, classical or quantum (Section 4). Quantum proposals are untested at the level of living brains. |

### 4A.7 Quantum proposals about consciousness and their relationship to other theories

- **Orch OR** is the best-known quantum theory of consciousness. It links conscious moments to objective collapse in microtubules. It differs from most theories in Section 4, which describe mechanisms in classical terms (broadcasting, recurrent processing, higher-order representation, and integrated cause–effect structure).
- **Consciousness-causes-collapse interpretations.** Some interpretations of quantum mechanics, proposed in the twentieth century, say a conscious observer collapses the state. These are minority interpretations. Standard physics treats "measurement" as any suitable physical interaction, and decoherence calculations do not require an observer with a mind.
- **Quantum Zeno and attention.** Henry Stapp has proposed that repeated mental attention could hold neural states through the quantum Zeno effect. It is a conceptual proposal with no direct test in brains.
- **Relation to IIT.** IIT in its standard form is defined for classical causal systems. Extensions of IIT to quantum systems have been proposed theoretically, but they are not part of the tests that the adversarial study in Section 4 carried out.
- **Relation to the adversarial test.** The Cogitate study compared GNWT and IIT, both classical-mechanism theories, so it does not address the quantum question directly.
- **Even if quantum effects exist,** they would not automatically explain consciousness. A quantum mechanism would still need an account of why and how any physical process is accompanied by experience, which is the explanatory gap described in Section 15. Replacing one unexplained thing with another does not close it.

### 4A.8 What would change the picture

**Evidence that would strengthen quantum proposals**
- Direct measurement of coherence or entanglement in intact living neurons, with controls for heating and scattering artifacts
- Independent replication of isotope effects on behavior and anesthesia (lithium and xenon), with spin-dependent predictions confirmed
- Replication of the microtubule-drug anesthesia result across species, together with evidence that the effect is quantum rather than structural
- Shielding or perturbation experiments that alter a proposed quantum effect and change behavior in a predicted way
- A model in which quantum effects solve a computational task better than a classical neural model of equal resources

**Evidence that would weaken them**
- Failure of replication of the isotope and microtubule findings
- Classical models that fully reproduce the anesthetic and behavioral effects
- Measured coherence times in living tissue far shorter than any proposed mechanism requires
- A direct test of the proposed gravity-related collapse mechanism that rules it out

### 4A.9 How to evaluate "quantum brain" claims

Section 16 gives a general checklist. For quantum claims, also ask:
1. Does the claim distinguish between "the brain obeys quantum mechanics" (true of everything) and "the brain uses quantum coherence for computation" (unproven)?
2. Is the evidence from isolated molecules in a dish, from animals, or from living human brains?
3. Does the proposed mechanism address the decoherence timescale problem with numbers, or only by analogy?
4. Does the claim reach for "quantum" as a label for something not yet understood, such as consciousness, free will, or healing? A mysterious phenomenon plus a mysterious theory is not an explanation.
5. Is a product, therapy, or "quantum brain training" being sold? Such claims have no established evidence.
6. Can the claim be tested, and would a negative result be accepted?

**Bottom line**
- Quantum mechanics is essential to the brain as a physical object, but this does not show that neurons compute quantum-mechanically.
- Established quantum effects in other living systems make the brain question legitimate, not proven.
- Current experimental findings, such as microtubule drug effects on anesthesia and tryptophan superradiance, are intriguing and are consistent with some quantum proposals, but they do not demonstrate quantum consciousness.
- The mainstream view treats brain function as classical at the level of neurons and circuits, with the quantum-mind view remaining a minority hypothesis that is worth testing.

Sources: [Tegmark, "The importance of quantum decoherence in brain processes," *Physical Review E* (2000), arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/9907009v1); [Wellesley College report on the microtubule-stabilizing drug and anesthesia study, published in *eNeuro* (2024)](https://www.wellesley.edu/news/wellesley-teams-new-research-on-anesthesia-unlocks-important-clues-about-the-nature-of-consciousness); [Gassab and Pusuluk, review of quantum models of the brain, including the Posner model (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.03241v2); and Hagan, Hameroff, and Tuszynski, *Physical Review E* (2002), for the reply to Tegmark. Treat preprints, news summaries, and popular science write-ups as pointers to the primary literature, not as established findings.

## 5. What brain science can measure—and what its tools cannot

| Method | What it measures | Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **MRI / structural MRI** | Anatomy and tissue properties | Detailed noninvasive structure | Does not directly show thoughts or moment-to-moment neural firing. |
| **fMRI** | Blood-oxygenation changes related indirectly to neural activity | Whole-brain coverage and useful spatial detail | Slow relative to neural firing; correlation is not causation; motion and analysis choices matter. |
| **EEG** | Scalp electrical potentials | Millisecond timing, relatively affordable | Source localization is difficult; signals mix activity from many sources. |
| **MEG** | Magnetic fields generated by neural currents | Fast timing; useful source modeling | Expensive and technically demanding; not equally sensitive to all sources. |
| **PET** | Tracer-based metabolism, blood flow, or molecular targets | Can examine selected molecular processes | Radiation exposure, cost, and lower temporal resolution. |
| **Intracranial EEG / single-unit recordings** | Electrical activity from electrodes inside the skull or brain | High temporal resolution; can directly sample local activity | Invasive, limited coverage, usually available only in clinical contexts. |
| **Lesion studies** | Effects associated with damaged tissue | Can help establish causal necessity | Lesions are rarely neat; reorganization and network effects complicate interpretation. |
| **TMS / electrical stimulation** | Perturbation of neural tissue | Can test causal contributions to behavior and symptoms | Effects vary by site, dose, state, and individual; stimulation is not a simple on/off switch. |
| **Connectomics** | Anatomical connections between cells or regions | Maps potential routes for communication | A wiring diagram does not alone reveal what signals mean or when connections are used. |
| **Computational models / AI** | Formal hypotheses about learning, dynamics, and behavior | Makes assumptions explicit and permits simulations | A model can fit data without being the brain's actual mechanism. |

**Consciousness relevance:** no scan is a direct "consciousness meter." Researchers combine behavior, physiology, reports, perturbation, and longitudinal observations. Neural decoding can infer some information under constrained conditions, but it should not be confused with unrestricted mind reading.

## 6. Conditions that affect the brain

Brain disorders can arise from genetic, developmental, vascular, infectious, traumatic, degenerative, immune, metabolic, psychiatric, and environmental causes. Categories overlap, and a diagnosis does not imply one uniform mechanism.

| Category | Examples | Brain-related issues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neurodegenerative | Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease | Progressive changes in cells, proteins, circuits, movement, cognition, or behavior. |
| Vascular | Stroke, vascular cognitive impairment | Blood-flow disruption can rapidly injure tissue and alter movement, language, memory, or awareness. |
| Seizure disorders | Epilepsy | Abnormal synchronized activity can disrupt perception, movement, memory, and consciousness. |
| Psychiatric | Depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorders, PTSD, OCD, schizophrenia | Complex interactions among brain circuits, learning, body systems, environment, and lived experience; not reducible to a single chemical imbalance. |
| Neurodevelopmental | Autism, ADHD, intellectual disability | Differences in development and function; substantial individual variation and multiple contributing pathways. |
| Injury-related | Traumatic brain injury, concussion, spinal cord injury | Effects range from temporary symptoms to persistent changes in cognition, mood, movement, and awareness. |
| Sleep-related | Insomnia, sleep apnea, circadian disorders | Can affect attention, mood, memory, cardiovascular health, and day-to-day functioning. |

**Consciousness relevance:** disorders can affect content, level, continuity, and reportability of experience differently. A person's outward behavior is an imperfect window into their inner state.

## 7. Treatments and interventions: benefits, trade-offs, and evidence

There is no universal brain-enhancement treatment. Effectiveness depends on the target condition, severity, individual biology, dose, duration, access, and whether outcomes are measured in a meaningful way. Treatments should be chosen with qualified clinicians, not self-experimentation.

| Intervention | Common use or proposed benefit | Known trade-offs and limits | Consciousness connection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sleep and circadian regularity** | Supports attention, learning, mood, metabolic health, and memory processing | Needs vary; sleep disorders may require medical assessment; excessive sleep can also signal illness | Sleep changes the level and content of experience, including dreaming. |
| **Physical activity** | Evidence supports benefits for general health, mood, and some cognitive outcomes; helps manage vascular risk | Injury, overtraining, and barriers to access; effects on specific cognitive skills are not guaranteed | Mood and bodily state shape experience; exercise is not a direct consciousness treatment. |
| **Learning, social engagement, and cognitive rehabilitation** | Builds skills, compensatory strategies, and functional independence | Gains may be task-specific; transfer to unrelated abilities is variable | Can change what a person can attend to, remember, and report. |
| **Psychotherapy** | Effective for several mental-health conditions; helps change patterns of thought, behavior, emotion, and coping | Access, time, fit with therapist, and temporary distress; no single therapy works for everyone | Changes interpretation and awareness of mental processes without requiring direct stimulation. |
| **Prescription psychiatric medicines** | Can reduce symptoms of depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, psychosis, ADHD, and other conditions | Benefits and side effects vary; may include sleep, appetite, sexual, metabolic, movement, or emotional effects; stopping some abruptly can be risky | Can alter attention, mood, alertness, and subjective experience. "Chemical imbalance" is an oversimplification. |
| **Anesthetic and sedative medicines** | Enable surgery, procedures, and selected acute care | Respiratory and cardiovascular risks, delirium in vulnerable patients, impaired memory; require clinical monitoring | Can alter or abolish responsiveness; the exact relationship between responsiveness and experience is complex. |
| **Antiseizure medicines** | Reduce seizure frequency for many people | Side effects can include fatigue, dizziness, cognitive or mood changes; effectiveness differs | Seizures may alter awareness; treatment aims to prevent harmful episodes, not enhance consciousness. |

| **Anti-amyloid therapies (lecanemab, donanemab)** | In eligible people with early Alzheimer's, trials show a modest slowing of decline on average, not a cure or reversal | Brain swelling/bleeding risks (ARIA), monitoring MRI requirements, cost/access burdens, and careful eligibility assessment | Intended to preserve function and continuity over time; benefits and risks must be weighed individually. |
| **Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** | Can be highly effective for severe depression and some other serious conditions, especially when rapid response is needed | Requires anesthesia; confusion and memory side effects can occur, including autobiographical memory problems in some patients | Induces a controlled seizure and changes brain network function; does not imply a simple theory of consciousness. |
| **Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)** | Noninvasive treatment for selected conditions, especially treatment-resistant depression; other uses depend on the device and its regulatory clearance | Repeated sessions; headache/scalp discomfort; rare seizure risk; response varies | Modulates circuits without general anesthesia; does not directly reveal or control conscious content. |
| **Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)** | Implanted VNS is used for selected epilepsy and depression indications; noninvasive devices have specific indications | Surgery/device maintenance for implants; voice changes, cough, discomfort; benefit can take time | Alters brainstem-linked neuromodulatory systems; effects on awareness are indirect. |
| **Deep brain stimulation (DBS)** | Established for selected movement disorders; studied for other conditions | Brain surgery, infection/bleeding risks, programming burden, variable effects on mood/cognition; not a general-purpose enhancer | Stimulation can change movement, mood, and behavior; psychiatric uses remain more limited/experimental depending on indication. |
| **Stroke rehabilitation and neurorehabilitation** | Repetitive, goal-directed practice and assistive technology can improve function after injury | Recovery varies; intensive therapy can be tiring; gains may plateau and access is unequal | Helps restore communication and agency; behavior alone may not reveal all residual awareness. |
| **Brain–computer interfaces (BCIs)** | Experimental or emerging systems can decode intended movement or speech for some people with paralysis | Invasive surgery for some systems, limited reliability, setup burden, privacy/security issues, and uncertain long-term durability | Can provide a communication channel when ordinary speech or movement is unavailable; decoding intent is not the same as reading private thoughts. |
| **Psychedelic-assisted therapy and other emerging treatments** | Research explores selected psychiatric indications and mechanisms of learning/plasticity | Legal and clinical status vary; intense psychological effects; risks for some psychiatric conditions; uncertain durability; need for structured care | Can profoundly alter experience; altered consciousness is not itself proof of therapeutic benefit. |
| **Supplements and "nootropics"** | Some correct deficiencies or have specific evidence-based uses | Many marketed claims are weak; contamination, interactions, dose uncertainty, and side effects are possible | Claims of enhancing awareness or intelligence often exceed evidence. |

### Trade-offs in "brain enhancement"

- **Performance versus well-being:** pushing alertness or work hours can degrade sleep, mood, learning, and health.
- **Short-term benefit versus long-term uncertainty:** a drug or device may improve a task while having poorly understood long-term effects.
- **Specificity versus generality:** interventions that improve one function may not improve unrelated abilities.
- **Plasticity versus stability:** the ability to change supports learning but also makes harmful learning and maladaptive habits possible.
- **Symptom relief versus underlying cause:** a treatment may improve function without stopping disease progression.
- **Average effect versus individual response:** trial averages do not predict a particular person's outcome.
- **Access versus innovation:** high costs, specialist availability, geography, and insurance rules determine who benefits.
- **Measurement versus lived experience:** a change in a brain scan or test score may not translate into a meaningful improvement in daily life.

## 9. Technologies that may change neuroscience

### Better brain maps

Cell atlases and connectomes aim to identify what cell types exist, where they are located, how they connect, and how they behave. A map is foundational but incomplete: the same wiring can behave differently depending on activity, neuromodulation, learning, and body state.

### Recording and manipulating activity

New electrodes, optical methods, molecular sensors, and noninvasive imaging can record more cells or larger networks. Optogenetics and other cell-targeted techniques are powerful in animal research, but translating them safely and effectively to humans is a separate challenge.

**Consciousness relevance:** the ability to perturb a circuit and observe a change in perception or responsiveness can provide stronger causal evidence than observation alone. Ethical constraints limit which experiments can be performed in humans.

### Brain–computer interfaces

BCIs can translate selected neural patterns into cursor movements, text, speech, or device commands. The goal is often restoration of communication or movement, not unrestricted reading of thoughts. Systems differ widely: some are invasive, some noninvasive, and many remain research-stage.

Important limitations include signal drift, training burden, surgical risks, data security, device maintenance, and unequal access. Neural signals are context-dependent; a decoder trained for one task or person may not generalize.

### NeuroAI and computational neuroscience

AI can help analyze high-dimensional neural data, segment microscopy images, infer cell types, model circuits, and compare hypotheses. Neuroscience can in turn inspire learning systems that adapt efficiently, learn from limited experience, and operate under energy constraints.

A model's success at predicting data does not prove that the brain uses the same algorithm. Useful models should make risky, testable predictions and be compared against simpler alternatives.

### Digital twins and personalized brain models

Researchers aspire to create individualized computational models of anatomy, connectivity, physiology, and disease. Such models could eventually aid surgical planning or treatment selection. At present, a complete personal "brain simulation" capable of reproducing a person's memories, personality, or consciousness is not an established clinical capability.

## 10. Brain health: evidence-based foundations and limits

Broadly useful habits include adequate sleep, regular physical activity, treatment of vascular risk factors, avoiding smoking and harmful substance use, wearing appropriate head protection, maintaining social and cognitive engagement, and seeking care for persistent mood, sleep, memory, or neurological symptoms. Hearing and vision problems, medication effects, and other medical conditions can also influence cognitive performance.

These are risk-reduction and health-support measures, not guarantees against disease. "Brain training" apps may improve practiced tasks, but broad transfer to general intelligence or everyday functioning is much less certain. Supplements marketed for memory or focus often have weaker evidence than advertising suggests.

**Consciousness relevance:** healthy brain function supports wakefulness, attention, emotional regulation, and the capacity to engage with experience, but no lifestyle intervention has been shown to unlock a hidden, unlimited level of consciousness.


## 11. Exercises and activities: how practice may affect brain systems

This section turns the preceding neuroscience into a practical, evidence-aware menu of activities. The aim is not to “activate” one brain region in isolation. Nearly every real task recruits multiple, interacting networks, and the same activity can affect different people differently. The links below are **functional associations**, not a claim that an exercise selectively trains a single structure or that a subjective feeling proves a specific neural change.

**Evidence guide**
- **Well supported:** the activity is broadly supported for the stated outcome, although the size of the effect varies.
- **Promising / task-specific:** studies suggest benefits for some outcomes, but transfer to unrelated skills is uncertain.
- **Exploratory:** a plausible research or self-observation activity, not an established way to improve a brain system.

These activities are general education, not treatment for a neurological or psychiatric condition. Start at a comfortable level, adapt for disability or injury, and stop if an activity causes pain, dizziness, panic, or other concerning symptoms. People with medical limitations should ask a qualified clinician what forms of exercise are appropriate. Do not use sleep deprivation, extreme exertion, deliberate oxygen restriction, unsupervised brain stimulation, or psychoactive substances as “brain exercises.”

### 11.1 Movement, coordination, and whole-brain health

| **Name the distraction and return to the task** (“planning,” “remembering,” “worrying”) | Attention, language, metacognitive monitoring, and emotion regulation networks | Awareness of thought patterns and deliberate redirection | **Promising / task-specific.** Use neutral labels; the goal is observation, not suppressing thoughts or judging yourself. | Relates to higher-order and attention-schema accounts because a person represents aspects of their own mental activity. Such self-report remains imperfect evidence about the underlying mechanism of consciousness. |
| **Task switching with an error log**: alternate between two simple tasks and track mistakes and time | Executive-control and attention networks | Better understanding of switching costs and personal limits; practice may help with the task rules | **Well supported** that switching has costs; improvement in general multitasking is not guaranteed. Compare single-task and alternating-task performance. | Makes the cost of changing goals observable. A conscious intention to switch does not mean all switching processes are conscious. |
| **Pause–predict–check**: before a choice, write your prediction and confidence, then compare with the outcome | Executive control, memory, valuation, and error-monitoring systems | Calibration, planning, and learning from feedback | **Promising / task-specific.** Keep predictions measurable and record outcomes without rewriting the original estimate. | Supports examination of metacognition and the accuracy of conscious confidence. A feeling of certainty is not a direct readout of neural accuracy or subjective experience. |

### 11.4 Emotion, stress, interoception, and social cognition

| Exercise | Systems likely involved | What may improve | Evidence and practical approach | Consciousness and theory connections |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Slow, comfortable breathing** without breath-holding or forcing depth | Autonomic regulation, brainstem-linked systems, interoceptive networks, and attention | May help some people settle acute stress or arousal | **Moderate, protocol-dependent evidence.** Breathe comfortably and stop if light-headed. This is not a substitute for care for persistent anxiety or breathing symptoms. | Changes bodily signals that contribute to felt emotion and arousal. Embodied theories emphasize these loops; subjective calm alone does not identify a specific neural mechanism. |
| **Emotion diary with situation, body cues, interpretation, and action urge** | Memory, language, interoception, emotion-related networks, and executive monitoring | Better recognition of patterns and triggers; may support therapy | **Promising as a self-monitoring aid**, not a standalone treatment for every condition. Keep entries brief and nonjudgmental; discontinue if journaling intensifies rumination. | Separates bodily changes, interpretations, and felt experience—components that theories of emotion and consciousness try to explain. A diary is a report, not a direct neural measurement. |
| **Cognitive reappraisal**: write one initial interpretation, then two plausible alternatives supported by evidence | Prefrontal control networks interacting with emotion and memory systems | Flexible interpretation and coping in some situations | **Moderate, context-dependent evidence.** Do not use reappraisal to deny danger, excuse abuse, or dismiss legitimate emotions. | Tests how interpretation changes felt meaning. It aligns with the idea that context and prior beliefs shape experience, without proving any single predictive-processing theory. |
| **Active listening**: summarize another person's point before replying; ask whether you understood correctly | Language, attention, memory, perspective-taking, and social-cognition networks | Listening accuracy and communication skills | **Promising / skill-specific.** Practice in ordinary conversations with consent and respect. | Demonstrates that another person's experience is inferred rather than directly accessed. Social judgments about awareness connect to attention-schema ideas, but cannot establish another person's private experience with certainty. |
| **Graded, supported exposure to a safe feared situation** | Learning and memory systems, threat/relevance networks, attention, and regulation systems | May reduce avoidance and update learned threat expectations in appropriate clinical contexts | **Evidence-based within suitable treatments**, but not appropriate for every fear or trauma without careful planning. For severe anxiety, PTSD, panic, or trauma-related symptoms, work with a qualified clinician rather than forcing exposure alone. | Changes how cues are interpreted and experienced; it does not erase the reality of a person's feelings. Conscious fear, physiological arousal, and learned responses may change at different rates. |

### 11.5 Sensory perception, prediction, and body awareness

| Exercise | Systems likely involved | What may improve | Evidence and practical approach | Consciousness and theory connections |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mindful sensory scan**: identify a few sounds, colors, textures, or smells in the environment | Sensory pathways, attention, memory, and salience networks | Present-moment noticing and attentional practice | **Promising / task-specific.** Briefly name observations without needing to judge them. Avoid if sensory focus is uncomfortable. | Makes the distinction between sensory processing and deliberate awareness easier to notice. GNWT, RPT, and other theories disagree about what additional processing makes sensory information conscious. |
| **Perceptual discrimination practice**: compare similar sounds, shapes, or tones and receive feedback | Task-specific sensory cortex and associated attention/decision networks | Accuracy for the practiced discrimination | **Well supported for many trained perceptual tasks**, with transfer varying by task. Use safe sound levels and avoid visual or auditory strain. | Shows that perception can become more precise through learning. Improved discrimination does not show that a person has gained more consciousness overall. |
| **Expectation-versus-observation log**: predict what a faint or ambiguous stimulus will be, then record what is actually identifiable | Sensory, attention, memory, and decision systems | Awareness of expectation and uncertainty; not necessarily better raw perception | **Exploratory.** Use ordinary, harmless stimuli and include “uncertain” as a valid response. | A practical way to explore predictive-processing ideas and perceptual bias. Results are not a clinical test and do not uniquely support one theory. |
| **Eyes-closed body-position task while seated**: gently identify the position of a hand or foot, then check visually | Proprioceptive pathways, somatosensory cortex, and body representation networks | Awareness of body-position cues and task-specific discrimination | **Promising / task-specific.** Stay seated, use gentle movements, and avoid if you have impaired sensation or injury without professional advice. | Highlights how bodily signals contribute to the experienced body. It does not establish that the self is merely a body model or settle debates about self-consciousness. |

### 11.6 Sleep, recovery, and routines that support learning

| Exercise or habit | Systems likely involved | What may improve | Evidence and practical approach | Consciousness and theory connections |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Keep a consistent sleep–wake schedule** as far as practical | Circadian timing systems, hypothalamic and brainstem sleep–wake networks, and widespread cortical systems | Alertness, mood, learning, and regularity of sleep | **Well supported** as a general sleep-health practice. Most adults need about 7–9 hours, but needs vary. Persistent insomnia, loud snoring, or daytime sleepiness warrants medical evaluation. | Sleep changes responsiveness and the content of experience, including dreams. Sleep and anesthesia research inform consciousness science but do not by themselves identify a complete theory. |
| **Review learned material after sleep rather than replacing sleep with cramming** | Memory systems and distributed cortical networks | Retention and consolidation for many kinds of learning | **Well supported**, though sleep's effects differ by memory type and individual. Study, sleep adequately, and test recall later. | A person may have conscious experiences during sleep, but later recall is incomplete and cannot perfectly reveal what was experienced. |
| **Schedule recovery breaks** after demanding mental or physical work | Attention, arousal, and task-dependent networks | May help sustain performance and reduce fatigue | **Practical, individual-dependent.** Use breaks that actually provide recovery rather than adding another demanding screen task. | Fatigue changes attention and the experience of effort. It should not be mistaken for a simple global decrease in consciousness. |

### 11.7 Exercises mapped to brain components

This is a simplified cross-reference. It describes systems commonly involved, not exclusive targets.

| Brain component or system | Activities that commonly recruit it | Important caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hippocampus / medial temporal memory systems** | Spaced retrieval, learning routes, remembering stories, contextual learning | Remembering a task better does not mean all memory systems have improved. |
| **Prefrontal and frontoparietal control networks** | Planning, single-task focus, task switching, explaining a concept, checking predictions | “Executive function” is distributed; a single puzzle does not isolate or fully train the prefrontal cortex. |
| **Sensory and parietal networks** | Perceptual discrimination, drawing from observation, hand–eye tasks, body-position tasks | Sensory practice often improves the practiced discrimination more than unrelated abilities. |
| **Cerebellum** | Balance, dance, coordinated movement, timing and error-correction tasks | It contributes to more than movement, but a movement drill is not a proven way to improve every cognitive function associated with the cerebellum. |
| **Basal ganglia and action-learning loops** | Repeated skill practice, habit formation, sequencing, feedback-guided movement | Repetition can strengthen helpful skills and unhelpful habits alike; design the routine deliberately. |
| **Amygdala and broader relevance/threat networks** | Emotion learning, safe and supported exposure, reappraisal, emotionally meaningful learning | The amygdala is not just a fear center; activities are not a substitute for treatment of trauma or anxiety disorders. |
| **Hypothalamus, brainstem, and autonomic systems** | Sleep regularity, suitable exercise, comfortable breathing, hydration and routine | These systems regulate vital functions. Do not attempt extreme breathing, oxygen restriction, or sleep deprivation as training. |
| **White matter and long-range network communication** | Repeated learning and coordinated skills over time | A person cannot directly feel or verify that a specific white-matter pathway has changed without appropriate measurement. |
| **Neurons, synapses, and glia** | Learning, sleep, physical activity, rehabilitation, and experience across time | These cellular components are not consciously targeted by a specific everyday exercise; changes are complex and cannot be inferred from a feeling or score alone. |

### 11.8 How activities relate to theories of consciousness

Exercises can help people notice attention, perception, bodily signals, memory, and self-report. **They cannot, by themselves, prove a theory of consciousness.** The theories in Section 4 make different claims about mechanisms that may be necessary for experience; testing those claims requires carefully controlled experiments and measurements.

| Theory or framework | Relevant activity or observation | What it may illustrate | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT)** | Learn information, then test whether it can be flexibly used across tasks; compare focused attention with distraction | How information becomes available for report, planning, and flexible behavior | Flexible report is not proof of a unique global “ignition” mechanism; task performance alone cannot validate GNWT. |
| **Integrated Information Theory (IIT)** | Compare how integrated a task or system appears across different conditions, using only established behavioral observations | Raises questions about unity and integration | Everyday exercises cannot measure IIT's proposed quantities or establish that a particular activity increases consciousness. |
| **Recurrent Processing Theory (RPT)** | Compare rapid first impressions with later feedback, correction, and repeated viewing | Shows that perception can change with ongoing processing and feedback | Subjective reports cannot isolate recurrent neural processing from attention, memory, and decision-making. |
| **Higher-Order Theories (HOT)** | Record an initial judgment and a separate confidence rating; compare confidence with accuracy | Makes metacognitive monitoring observable | Confidence is not the same as consciousness, and a diary cannot show whether higher-order representation is necessary for experience. |
| **Predictive-processing accounts** | Make a prediction about an ambiguous stimulus, observe it, and log prediction errors | Shows how expectations can influence interpretation and be revised | This does not show that every brain function is predictive coding or that prediction alone explains subjective experience. |
| **Attention schema theory** | Notice how you infer that another person is paying attention, then check your inference with them | Highlights that judgments about attention and awareness can be models rather than direct access | Does not prove that an attention model creates subjective experience. |
| **Enactive / embodied approaches** | Practice balance, movement, sensory exploration, and comfortable interoceptive awareness | Demonstrates that action, bodily state, and environment shape perception and feeling | Does not prove that bodily interaction is sufficient to explain all consciousness. |
| **Physicalist views** | Observe how sleep, injury, medication, fatigue, and sensory changes alter experience and performance | Fits the strong evidence that brain and body states systematically affect experience | Dependence on brain states does not, on its own, complete the explanation of why experience feels like anything. |
| **Dualist or non-physical views** | Reflect on the difference between first-person experience and third-person measurement | Clarifies the philosophical question of subjective experience | Personal reflection is not empirical evidence for a non-physical mechanism and does not replace testable predictions. |

### 11.9 A practical four-week starter plan

The plan below is a flexible example, not a prescription. Choose activities that are safe and accessible for you. The purpose is to build a sustainable routine and observe meaningful outcomes—not to diagnose brain function or measure consciousness.

| Week | Main focus | Example routine | What to record |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1: Establish a baseline** | Sleep regularity, comfortable movement, and one short learning task | Keep a simple sleep log; do suitable movement on several days; use 5–10 minutes of spaced retrieval | Sleep duration/regularity, energy, mood, and recall accuracy |
| **2: Add attention practice** | Single-task intervals and gentle attention redirection | One focused work interval on most days; briefly note distractions and return to the task | Completed work, interruptions, and perceived effort |
| **3: Add coordination or perception** | A safe movement skill or a perceptual task | Practice balance with support, dance steps, drawing, or sound/shape discrimination for short sessions | Accuracy, consistency, and any discomfort |
| **4: Add reflection and compare** | Metacognition and review | Use a prediction-and-check log; compare results with Week 1; keep the most sustainable activities | Observable changes, barriers, and whether the activity helped daily life |

**Simple tracking template**
- Activity and duration:
- Sleep / fatigue before the activity:
- Goal or prediction:
- Observable result (time, accuracy, recall, or completion):
- Mood or discomfort, if relevant:
- What changed when I repeated it?
- What else might explain the result (practice, sleep, stress, familiarity, or chance)?

Do not infer that a brain region changed simply because a score improved. Improvement can reflect familiarity, strategy, motivation, measurement noise, or several interacting mechanisms. For a stronger personal experiment, define one outcome in advance, use the same measure over time, avoid changing many things at once, and treat the result as an observation rather than a diagnosis.

### 11.10 What “brain exercise” cannot currently promise

- No everyday activity is known to selectively strengthen one brain region while leaving the rest uninvolved.
- Improvement on a practiced task does not reliably imply a broad increase in intelligence, memory, attention, or creativity.
- There is no established exercise routine proven to increase the amount of consciousness a person has, unlock unlimited awareness, or settle the competing theories.
- A change in mood, attention, or self-awareness is meaningful as an experience, but it is not a direct measurement of neurons, synapses, or a theory-specific consciousness mechanism.
- Rehabilitation after injury or treatment for a disorder may require individualized, clinician-guided exercises. General brain-health habits should complement—not replace—appropriate care.

The strongest practical approach is to combine **regular suitable physical activity, sufficient sleep, purposeful learning, social connection, and task-specific practice**, then judge success by sustained function and well-being rather than by claims about “activating” a particular brain area.


## 12. Twenty-five everyday tasks and what happens in the brain

This section describes 25 common tasks and, in simplified form, which brain systems are typically involved and what they appear to be doing. It builds on the structures in Section 1 and the multilevel view in Section 3. The tasks are grouped into five categories: perception and movement, memory and learning, thinking and decision-making, emotion and social life, and body and brain states.

**How to read the tables**
- Each description names systems that are *commonly* involved, based on a mix of human imaging, lesion, and recording studies and, in some cases, animal research. It is not a complete circuit diagram.
- No task uses one region in isolation. Regions act as parts of networks, and the balance of activity shifts with practice, fatigue, age, and individual differences.
- Much of the processing described here happens without conscious awareness. Awareness of the *result* (a recognized face, a remembered event, a feeling of pain) is a separate question, discussed in Section 4.
- The "Notes and caveats" column flags simplifications, debates, and the limits of the evidence. These are educational summaries, not diagnostic statements about any person's brain.

### 12.1 Perception and movement

| # | Task | What happens in the brain | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Reading a sentence** | Light patterns on the retina are relayed through the thalamus to primary visual cortex. Eye movements are directed by midbrain and frontal systems so that each word lands on the sharpest part of the retina. A region of left ventral occipitotemporal cortex, often called the visual word form area, helps recognize letter strings. Left-lateralized temporal and frontal language networks then link words to sounds, meanings, and grammar, while working memory in prefrontal and parietal cortex holds the sentence so far. | The exact role and name of the word-form region are debated. Skilled reading is fast and largely automatic, and the same networks are reused for listening in part. |
| 2 | **Recognizing a face** | Visual information flows along the ventral visual stream, where face-selective patches in occipital and temporal cortex (including the fusiform and superior temporal regions) analyze facial features and expression. Anterior temporal regions contribute to identity and what you know about the person, while memory systems and the amygdala add familiarity and emotional significance. | Face-selective regions also respond to other stimuli, and face recognition is a network process rather than a single module. Damage can cause prosopagnosia (difficulty recognizing faces) with other vision intact. |
| 3 | **Following a conversation in a noisy room** | Sound is converted to neural signals in the cochlea and relayed through brainstem and thalamus to auditory cortex. Frontoparietal attention networks bias processing toward the attended voice, and cortical activity tracks the speech rhythm of that voice more strongly than competing sounds. Language areas extract meaning, and visual cues such as lip movements are combined with sound in multisensory regions. | Hearing loss can make this far more effortful because additional attention and memory resources are needed. Listening effort and fatigue are real and measurable. |
| 4 | **Catching a ball** | Visual motion areas and parietal cortex estimate the ball's trajectory, and the brain predicts where and when it will arrive instead of reacting only to its current position. Premotor and motor cortex plan the reach, the cerebellum refines timing and coordination, and spinal circuits carry out the movement. Sensory feedback from the hand and arm lets the cerebellum and cortex correct errors over repeated attempts. | Much of this is not consciously controlled. People can catch objects without being able to describe the calculation involved. |
| 5 | **Typing without looking** | Early in learning, prefrontal and attention networks are heavily involved. With practice, basal ganglia and cerebellar circuits help turn sequences of keystrokes into smoother, more automatic chunks that motor and premotor cortex execute, with somatosensory feedback and error-monitoring checking the result. | The shift from effortful to automatic control is a useful generalization, not a clean handoff between regions. Control networks still engage when errors occur or the text is unfamiliar. |

### 12.2 Memory and learning

| # | Task | What happens in the brain | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 | **Holding a phone number in mind long enough to dial it** | Prefrontal and parietal cortex help maintain the information in working memory, aided by silent verbal rehearsal that uses speech- and language-related circuits. Maintenance may involve sustained activity, short-term changes in synaptic strength, or dynamic activity patterns depending on the task. | Working memory has strict capacity limits, and distraction can erase the contents. The underlying mechanisms are still debated. |
| 7 | **Recalling what you did yesterday** | The hippocampus and medial temporal lobe help reactivate distributed cortical patterns that were present when the event was experienced. Prefrontal regions guide the search and evaluate what comes to mind, while parietal and default-mode network regions are often active during autobiographical recall. | Retrieval is reconstructive: memories can change each time they are recalled, and vividness or confidence does not guarantee accuracy. |
| 8 | **Finding your way through a familiar neighborhood** | The hippocampus and neighboring entorhinal cortex contain cells that signal location, direction, and spatial structure (place cells, grid cells, and head-direction cells in animal research, with supporting evidence in humans). Retrosplenial and parietal cortex help connect landmarks to the direction you need to go, while basal ganglia circuits support habitual, turn-by-turn route responses. | Much of the cell-level detail comes from rodent studies. People vary in whether they rely more on map-like or route-based strategies. |
| 9 | **Practicing a new piece of music or other skill** | Auditory, motor, and somatosensory cortex work together while the cerebellum and basal ganglia refine timing, sequencing, and error correction. Repeated practice changes synaptic strength and the way motor cortex represents the skill. Sleep after practice is associated with consolidation of motor and other learning. | Practice quality, spacing, feedback, and sleep all matter. Skill-specific gains do not reliably transfer to unrelated abilities. |
| 10 | **Learning from a mistake** | Medial frontal regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex, are involved in detecting errors and conflicts. In reward-based learning, dopamine signals can encode the difference between what was expected and what happened (a prediction error), and basal ganglia and prefrontal circuits use this to adjust future choices. | Dopamine is one part of a larger learning system, and prediction error is not the only learning signal. Emotional state and context shape how feedback is used. |

### 12.3 Thinking and decision-making

| # | Task | What happens in the brain | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 11 | **Doing mental arithmetic** | Regions around the intraparietal sulcus contribute to representing quantity and magnitude. Prefrontal working-memory and control networks hold intermediate results and manage steps, and language-related circuits help retrieve memorized facts such as multiplication tables. | Strategy matters: memorized facts, counting, and estimation recruit somewhat different circuits. Anxiety about math can alter performance. |
| 12 | **Choosing between two options** | Ventromedial prefrontal cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, and the striatum contribute to signals about expected value. Parietal and frontal circuits appear to accumulate evidence over time until a choice is made, while the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and amygdala contribute information about conflict, cost, risk, bodily state, and emotional relevance. | There is no single "decision center." Decision models are simplified and differ across kinds of choices. |
| 13 | **Resisting a temptation** | Lateral prefrontal cortex and inferior frontal regions can bias valuation toward longer-term goals and help inhibit a prepotent response. They interact with striatal and other reward-related circuits that signal the pull of the immediate option. | Fatigue, stress, and poor sleep can weaken control. Claims that self-control is a simple depletable resource are contested, and changing the environment often works better than relying on in-the-moment effort. |
| 14 | **Having a sudden insight while solving a puzzle** | After an impasse, studies of insight problems report changes in the anterior cingulate cortex, temporal regions (including anterior right temporal regions), and memory systems that may reflect restructuring how the problem is represented. The "aha" moment can also engage reward-related circuits. | Findings come mainly from lab puzzles with modest sample sizes. How well they generalize to real-world creativity is uncertain. |
| 15 | **Switching between two tasks** | Frontoparietal control networks must reconfigure the goals and rules for the new task, while some activation from the old task lingers. This lingering contributes to slower responses and more errors right after a switch (switch cost). Basal ganglia circuits help gate which information is updated. | What is called multitasking is mostly rapid switching with costs. Practice can improve a specific task pair, but broad improvement in multitasking ability is not established. |

### 12.4 Emotion, motivation, and social life

| # | Task | What happens in the brain | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 16 | **Reacting to a sudden loud noise** | A fast brainstem circuit produces the startle reflex within tens of milliseconds. In parallel, sensory signals reach the amygdala and connected networks, including hypothalamic and brainstem regions, which drive autonomic changes such as faster heart rate and adrenaline release. Slower cortical and hippocampal processing evaluates the context and can dampen the response if there is no real danger. | The amygdala is not simply a fear center, and the relative importance of fast subcortical routes in humans is debated. A bodily alarm response is not identical to the felt experience of fear. |
| 17 | **Enjoying a favorite food or piece of music** | Reward-related circuits, including midbrain dopamine neurons, the ventral striatum, and orbitofrontal cortex, respond to valued experiences and help predict them. Opioid and endocannabinoid signaling contributes to the pleasure itself (liking), while dopamine is more closely tied to anticipation and motivation (wanting). Auditory or taste and smell pathways supply the sensory content. | Dopamine is not simply the pleasure chemical. Wanting and liking can be dissociated, and individual taste varies widely. |
| 18 | **Feeling pain after stubbing a toe** | Nociceptors send signals through the spinal cord to the thalamus. Somatosensory cortex contributes to where the pain is and how intense it is, while the insula and anterior cingulate cortex are more closely tied to its unpleasantness. Descending pathways from the prefrontal cortex and brainstem (including the periaqueductal gray) can amplify or reduce incoming signals. | Pain is not a direct readout of tissue damage and there is no single pain center. In chronic pain, plasticity in these circuits can maintain pain beyond the original injury. |
| 19 | **Reading another person's emotions** | Visual and auditory cortex process faces, body language, and tone of voice, with the superior temporal region and amygdala contributing to social and emotional signals. Networks involved in considering others' thoughts, including the temporoparietal junction and medial prefrontal cortex, help infer intentions, while insula and cingulate regions are implicated in sharing another person's feelings. | Inferences about others' states are imperfect and shaped by context and culture. Claims about "mirror neurons" explaining empathy in humans are debated. |
| 20 | **Speaking in a conversation** | Frontal and temporal networks select the meaning to express, left temporal regions help retrieve words, and inferior frontal and other regions sequence sounds and grammar. Motor cortex, cerebellum, and basal ganglia coordinate the lips, tongue, larynx, and breathing, while auditory cortex monitors your own voice so errors can be corrected. | The classic picture of two language areas is a simplification, since language is distributed across many regions. Handedness and individual differences affect lateralization. |

### 12.5 Body and brain states

| # | Task | What happens in the brain | Notes and caveats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 21 | **Falling asleep and dreaming** | Rising sleep pressure (including accumulation of adenosine) and circadian timing signals from the hypothalamus promote sleep-active neurons that inhibit arousal systems in the brainstem, hypothalamus, and basal forebrain. Thalamocortical activity changes, producing slow oscillations and sleep spindles in non-REM sleep. REM sleep involves brainstem-driven activation of the cortex with loss of muscle tone, and hippocampal-cortical interactions during sleep are linked to memory consolidation. | Dreams occur in REM but also in other sleep stages. The functions of sleep are partly understood, and sleep mechanisms differ across individuals and ages. |
| 22 | **Feeling hungry, then full** | The hypothalamus integrates hormonal signals such as ghrelin, leptin, and insulin with gut signals relayed through the vagus nerve to the brainstem. Specific hypothalamic neuron populations promote or suppress feeding, while reward, memory, and cognitive systems shape cravings and responses to food cues. | Much of the cell-level detail comes from animal studies. Eating is also shaped by learning, culture, social setting, and stress. |
| 23 | **Feeling more alert after coffee** | Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, reducing the effect of a molecule that builds up with time awake and promotes sleepiness. This indirectly increases activity in arousal-related and dopamine-related circuits. | Caffeine masks sleepiness but does not remove sleep debt. Sensitivity varies, late use can disrupt sleep, and it can worsen anxiety or palpitations in some people. |
| 24 | **Going for a run** | Motor cortex, cerebellum, and spinal rhythm-generating circuits produce and adjust the repeated stride pattern, while brainstem and autonomic systems adjust breathing and heart rate. Blood flow shifts, and neuromodulators and signaling molecules such as endocannabinoids and growth factors are released, which may contribute to changes in mood and plasticity. | Mechanisms behind the "runner's high" are debated, and some molecular findings come from animal studies. Responses vary with fitness, intensity, and health status. |
| 25 | **Letting your mind wander** | When attention is not tightly focused on an outside task, default-mode network regions (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate and precuneus, angular gyrus, and medial temporal areas) tend to become more active. They support autobiographical memory, imagining the future, and thinking about other people, and they interact with frontoparietal control networks that can sustain or interrupt the wandering. | The default-mode network is active in many kinds of tasks and is not simply "idle." Mind wandering can be useful for planning and creativity, but repetitive negative rumination is associated with distress. |

### 12.6 What these descriptions can and cannot tell us

- **Associations, not exclusive roles.** Imaging often reports average activity across groups of people. A region being active during a task does not mean it is sufficient or uniquely necessary for it.
- **Simplified timing.** These tasks unfold over milliseconds to minutes with feedback loops among regions, which a short description cannot capture.
- **Individual and state differences.** Age, training, sleep, stress, medications, and health conditions change which circuits are recruited and how efficiently.
- **Awareness is a separate question.** Most of the processing described here is not itself experienced. Which of these processes is accompanied by conscious experience, and why, is the question the theories in Section 4 try to answer.
- **No self-diagnosis.** Experiencing difficulty with a task such as reading, remembering, sleeping, or controlling impulses is not enough to infer anything about a specific brain region. Persistent or worsening difficulties are worth discussing with a qualified clinician.

## 13. Ethics, law, and society

### Mental privacy and neural data

Brain signals can reveal or permit inference about sensitive traits in specific contexts. That does not mean current systems can freely read arbitrary thoughts. Neurotechnology raises questions about consent, data ownership, security, secondary use, and whether neural data deserve special protections.

### Autonomy and enhancement

Brain stimulation, psychiatric medicines, and neuroprostheses can change mood, behavior, motivation, or agency. Ethical use requires informed consent, transparent discussion of uncertainty, respect for patient goals, and monitoring for unwanted effects.

### Disability and personhood

BCIs and rehabilitation should be developed with disabled people rather than merely for them. A person's worth is not measured by cognitive performance, speech, movement, or technological "normalization." Communication technology should expand agency and choice.

### Consciousness and moral status

Uncertainty about consciousness affects decisions involving infants, severe brain injury, dementia, anesthesia, animals, and future artificial systems. No simple behavioral checklist resolves all cases. Evidence should be assessed carefully, and uncertainty should encourage proportionate caution rather than confident speculation.

## 14. Persistent misconceptions

- **"We use only 10% of our brains."** False. Many brain systems are active across ordinary activities, though not every neuron fires at once.
- **"Left-brained people are logical; right-brained people are creative."** Oversimplified. Some functions are lateralized, but complex cognition uses both hemispheres.
- **"Dopamine is the pleasure chemical."** Incomplete. Dopamine participates in learning, motivation, movement, and prediction-related processes.
- **"The brain has ten times more glia than neurons."** Not supported by modern counts, which find roughly equal numbers of neurons and non-neuronal cells overall, with large differences between regions such as the cerebral cortex and the cerebellum.
- **"Glia are just support cells or glue."** Incomplete. Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, and related cells actively shape synapses, conduction speed, metabolism, immune defense, and blood flow, even though neurons remain the main cells for rapid signaling.
- **"Every memory is stored in one location."** False. Memory depends on distributed systems and can be reconstructed during retrieval.
- **"Brain imaging can read minds."** Current decoding is limited, task-dependent, probabilistic, and often requires extensive training and cooperation.
- **"A brain region has only one function."** False. Regions participate in multiple networks and functions.
- **"More neural activity is always better."** False. Function depends on appropriate patterns, timing, inhibition, and coordination.
- **"A treatment that changes a scan has necessarily helped."** False. Clinical benefit must be measured in meaningful outcomes, not only biomarkers.
- **"Higher levels of a neurotransmitter are always better."** False. Too much signaling by many transmitters causes harm, from serotonin syndrome to excitotoxicity, and most systems follow an inverted U (Section 2A).
- **"There is a gene for intelligence, aggression, or happiness."** Oversimplified. Most traits reflect thousands of variants of small effect plus environment. Single-gene syndromes with large effects exist but are the exception (Section 2C).
- **"Quantum physics explains consciousness."** Not established. Quantum mechanics underlies all chemistry, but evidence that quantum coherence or collapse plays a functional role in neurons is limited and contested (Section 4A).
- **"Science has proved consciousness is an illusion."** No. There are theories about how the brain constructs perceptions and self-models, but subjective experience is a real phenomenon requiring explanation, even if interpretations of it differ.

## 15. The biggest open questions

1. **The explanatory gap:** What complete account connects physical processes to subjective experience?
2. **The neural basis of conscious content:** Which circuits and dynamics are necessary or sufficient for particular experiences?
3. **Consciousness without report:** How can awareness be inferred when a person cannot communicate?
4. **Causal circuit models:** How do cell types and synaptic rules produce flexible behavior across contexts?
5. **Memory and identity:** How are memories stabilized, updated, and integrated into a changing self?
6. **Individual differences:** Why do people with similar diagnoses or injuries respond differently to treatment?
7. **Mental illness mechanisms:** How can research move from broad diagnostic categories to better mechanistic subtypes without reducing people to biomarkers?
8. **Neurodegeneration:** How can treatment prevent or halt disease, not only modestly slow symptoms or progression?
9. **Brain repair:** How can regeneration, plasticity, and neuroprosthetics restore function without harmful side effects?
10. **Ethical neurotechnology:** How can innovation protect privacy, autonomy, equity, and people whose ability to consent or communicate is limited?
11. **Cross-species inference:** Which findings from mice, primates, and artificial models generalize to human cognition and consciousness?
12. **What counts as explanation?** Can a theory predict and control brain behavior accurately while still leaving subjective experience unexplained?
13. **Quantum effects:** Do any non-trivial quantum processes contribute to neural computation or consciousness, and what experiments in living tissue could settle it?
14. **From genes to behavior:** How do thousands of small-effect variants, rare large-effect variants, and environment combine to shape brain development and behavior, and how can that knowledge be used without determinism?

## 16. How to evaluate future claims about the brain

When encountering a headline or product claim, ask:

1. Is the evidence from cells, animals, a small human study, a randomized trial, or routine clinical practice?
2. Was the study preregistered, adequately powered, independently replicated, and compared with an appropriate control?
3. Is the outcome meaningful to people, or merely a change in an imaging signal or test score?
4. How large is the effect, how long does it last, and who was studied?
5. What adverse effects, uncertainty, costs, and access barriers exist?
6. Does the claim distinguish correlation from causation?
7. Are the authors clear about limitations and alternative explanations?
8. Does the claim overreach from "associated with consciousness" to "explains consciousness"?

For consciousness theories specifically, look for predictions that distinguish one theory from its rivals. A theory that can explain every possible result after the fact is not a strong scientific theory.

## Final synthesis: where understanding stands

Neuroscience is not ignorant of the brain; it has accumulated a large and reliable body of knowledge. Scientists can map many structures and cell types, measure electrical and metabolic activity, identify circuits involved in perception and action, establish causal effects of lesions and stimulation, and treat a growing set of neurological and psychiatric conditions. Modern brain research is increasingly quantitative, collaborative, computational, and capable of connecting data across scales.

But a detailed inventory is not the same as a complete explanation. We do not yet have a finished account of how all these cells and circuits generate human-level intelligence, emotion, personal identity, or subjective experience. Even prominent consciousness theories face empirical challenges, and the best recent head-to-head test did not produce a decisive winner. Treatments can be powerful, but many are partial, condition-specific, and accompanied by real costs and risks. Emerging technologies—from cell atlases to BCIs—offer significant promise, while also creating new ethical obligations. Genetics and quantum physics show a similar pattern: strong findings at some levels (single-gene syndromes, quantum chemistry) and unresolved claims where they are stretched to explain behavior and consciousness.

The most accurate overall picture is **substantial progress without final understanding**. We know much more about what the brain does, which systems contribute, and how to measure and sometimes alter them than we know about why experience feels the way it does or how to predict every individual outcome. The path forward is not a single grand theory or miracle technology: it is careful experiments, reproducible data, causal tests, better models, inclusive clinical research, and honest treatment of uncertainty. Consciousness is not a decorative side topic in that effort; it is one of the clearest reminders of both the power and the limits of current brain science.

**18. (Section 4)** Detailed neural predictions: GNWT (ignition, broad availability), IIT (posterior "hot zone"), and recurrent processing theory (local recurrence in sensory cortex). Broader frameworks: predictive-processing accounts (a broad framework rather than one uniquely specified theory), enactive/embodied approaches (broad philosophical frameworks without decisive unique neural predictions), biological naturalism (more a philosophical position), and dualist views (hard to derive distinctive testable predictions). Higher-order and attention schema theories sit in between. The consequence is that the theories cannot be tested against each other equally easily: the more specific the neural predictions, the more directly they can be falsified.

**19. (Sections 4, 15, 16)** Finding what reliably accompanies an experience does not by itself explain why that activity has a subjective quality (the explanatory gap). The same warning appears in the open questions (the explanatory gap and "what counts as explanation"), in the measurement discussion (decoding is not mind reading), and in the evaluation checklist, which asks whether a claim overreaches from "associated with consciousness" to "explains consciousness."

**20. (Section 5)** Researchers combine behavior, physiology, reports, perturbation, and longitudinal observations. Neural decoding can infer some information under constrained conditions, but it should not be confused with unrestricted mind reading; the misconceptions section adds that decoding is limited, task-dependent, probabilistic, and often requires extensive training and cooperation.

**21. (Section 5)** (a) EEG, which offers millisecond timing and is relatively affordable; its limitation is that source localization is difficult and signals mix activity from many sources. MEG also offers fast timing but is expensive and technically demanding. fMRI is a poor fit because it measures blood-oxygenation changes that are slow relative to neural firing. (b) Lesion studies can help establish causal necessity, but lesions are rarely neat and reorganization complicates interpretation; TMS or electrical stimulation can test causal contributions, but effects vary by site, dose, state, and individual, and stimulation is not a simple on/off switch.

**22. (Sections 7, 9, 13)** Brain signals can reveal or permit inference about sensitive traits in specific contexts, which raises questions about consent, data ownership, security, secondary use, and whether neural data deserve special protections, even though current systems cannot freely read arbitrary thoughts. For autonomy, the document calls for informed consent, transparent discussion of uncertainty, respect for patient goals, and monitoring for unwanted effects. BCIs should be developed with disabled people rather than merely for them, and a person's worth is not measured by cognitive performance, speech, movement, or technological normalization. The BCI discussion also notes that decoding intent is not the same as reading private thoughts, and lists limitations such as signal drift, training burden, surgical risk, data security, maintenance, and unequal access.

**23. (Sections 7, 15)** Symptomatic medicines can temporarily help or stabilize selected cognitive and behavioral symptoms; effects are limited and variable, with possible gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, or other adverse effects, and they do not cure the disease. Anti-amyloid therapies (lecanemab, donanemab) show a modest slowing of decline on average in eligible people with early Alzheimer's, not a cure or reversal; risks and burdens include brain swelling or bleeding (ARIA), monitoring MRIs, cost and access barriers, and careful eligibility assessment. They illustrate the open question about neurodegeneration: how to prevent or halt disease rather than only modestly slow symptoms or progression.

**24. (Section 7)** ECT: requires anesthesia and induces a controlled seizure; can be highly effective for severe depression, but confusion and memory side effects (including autobiographical memory problems in some patients) can occur. rTMS: noninvasive; used for selected conditions, especially treatment-resistant depression; trade-offs are repeated sessions, headache or scalp discomfort, rare seizure risk, and variable response. DBS: involves brain surgery; established for selected movement disorders; risks include infection and bleeding, programming burden, and variable effects on mood and cognition. The document describes DBS as "not a general-purpose enhancer."

Does improvement on one test transfer to everyday memory (specificity versus generality, measurement versus lived experience)? How large and how durable is the effect, and who was studied (average versus individual response)? What adverse effects, interactions, dose uncertainty, or contamination exist (the supplements row)? Are long-term effects known (short-term benefit versus long-term uncertainty)? Does the claim distinguish correlation from causation, and are the authors clear about limitations? **26. (Sections 10, 11)** Habits listed: adequate sleep, regular physical activity, treating vascular risk factors, avoiding smoking and harmful substance use, appropriate head protection, social and cognitive engagement, and seeking care for persistent mood, sleep, memory, or neurological symptoms. Brain-training apps may improve practiced tasks, but broad transfer is much less certain, and supplements often have weaker evidence than advertising suggests. The later discussion adds that no everyday activity is known to selectively strengthen one region, practiced-task gains do not imply a broad increase in intelligence, memory, attention, or creativity, and success should be judged by sustained function and well-being.

**27. (Sections 4, 8)** Large programs often fund dozens or hundreds of investigators, so a single "main scientist" can misrepresent collaborative work. The document asks readers to distinguish an announced project, a preclinical result, an early clinical trial, and an approved treatment, and to treat "potential benefit" as a research goal rather than a demonstrated outcome. The Cogitate Consortium is designed to compare consciousness theories directly; its distinguishing features are a preregistered, adversarial comparison of predictions from GNWT and IIT using multiple methods (fMRI, MEG, and intracranial EEG).

**28. (Sections 8, 9)** A cell atlas identifies what cell types exist, where they are located, and how they behave (for example, reference atlases across species and lifespan), while a connectome maps anatomical connections between cells or regions. A map is foundational but incomplete: the same wiring can behave differently depending on activity, neuromodulation, learning, and body state, and a wiring diagram does not alone reveal what signals mean or when connections are used.

**29. (Section 9)** Optogenetics and other cell-targeted techniques are powerful in animal research, but translating them safely and effectively to humans is a separate challenge. Perturbing a circuit and observing a change in perception or responsiveness can provide stronger causal evidence than observation alone, though ethical constraints limit which experiments can be done in humans.

**30. (Section 9)** A useful model should make risky, testable predictions and be compared against simpler alternatives. Personalized digital twins are an aspiration that may eventually aid surgical planning or treatment selection; at present, a complete personal brain simulation that reproduces a person's memories, personality, or consciousness is not an established clinical capability.

**31. (Section 11)** Improvement can reflect familiarity, strategy, motivation, measurement noise, or several interacting mechanisms; the tracking template also lists practice, sleep, stress, and chance. A stronger experiment defines one outcome in advance, uses the same measure over time, avoids changing many things at once, and treats the result as an observation rather than a diagnosis.

**32. (Section 11)** Spaced retrieval practice: well supported. Single-task focus intervals: promising / task-specific. Expectation-versus-observation log: exploratory. Cognitive reappraisal: moderate, context-dependent evidence. "Well supported" means the activity is broadly supported for the stated outcome, although the effect size varies; it does not mean the benefit transfers to unrelated skills or that a particular brain region has been selectively trained.

**33. (Sections 1, 11, 12)** The cerebellum contributes to coordination, timing, motor learning, prediction, and some cognitive processes; in the everyday tasks it predicts and corrects timing (catching a ball) and refines sequencing (practicing music). The basal ganglia contribute to action selection, movement, habit learning, and reward-related learning, and support automatic sequences (typing) and habitual route responses. The warning is that the cerebellum contributes to more than movement, but a movement drill is not a proven way to improve every cognitive function associated with it; also, repetition can strengthen unhelpful habits as well as helpful skills.

**34. (Sections 3, 11, 12)** Sleep is an active biological state that supports health, learning, emotional regulation, and metabolic functions, and contributes to memory processing, though mechanisms and benefits differ across memory types. The habits section recommends reviewing material after sleep rather than cramming, and the everyday tasks link sleep after practice with consolidation and describe hippocampal-cortical interactions during sleep. Dreams are common in some sleep states but vivid experience can occur in others too (and the task section notes dreams occur outside REM). Anesthesia is a drug-induced change in brain dynamics acting on multiple targets, with different anesthetics working through partly different mechanisms; loss of responsiveness is not always identical to absence of experience.

**35. (Sections 1, 11, 12)** The amygdala is involved in learning about biologically significant events, threat, reward, and emotional relevance, so it is not simply a fear center. In the startle example, a fast brainstem reflex and parallel amygdala-linked circuits drive bodily arousal, while slower cortical and hippocampal processing evaluates context; a bodily alarm response is not identical to the felt experience of fear. In graded exposure, conscious fear, physiological arousal, and learned responses may change at different rates, and the activity does not erase the reality of a person's feelings. Severe anxiety, PTSD, panic, or trauma-related symptoms call for a qualified clinician rather than forcing exposure alone.

**36. (Sections 4, 5, 14, 16, 15)** Strong answers include several of: no scan is a "consciousness meter"; neural correlates are not a complete explanation; imaging has limitations (fMRI is indirect and slow; correlation is not causation); a brain region does not have only one function and consciousness depends on distributed systems and network interactions; decoding is not mind reading; "associated with" should not be inflated to "explains"; ask about study type, preregistration, power, replication, and controls; ask whether a scan change is meaningful to people; and the best head-to-head test of two major theories produced no decisive winner. Open questions overlooked include the explanatory gap, the neural basis of conscious content, consciousness without report, and "what counts as explanation."

**37. (Sections 2A, 2B)** Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter and the most abundant in the brain; it is released by pyramidal neurons, dentate granule cells, cerebellar granule cells, and thalamocortical neurons and supports fast signaling, plasticity, learning, and memory. GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter, released by interneurons, Purkinje cells, medium spiny neurons, and thalamic reticular neurons, and it shapes timing, gain, rhythms, and excitation–inhibition balance. Too little inhibition causes hyperexcitability: seizures, anxiety, tremor, insomnia (GABA), or exaggerated startle and stiffness (glycine in the brainstem and spinal cord). At the protein level, loss of one working copy of SCN1A reduces firing in inhibitory interneurons and causes Dravet syndrome, so networks become over-excitable. Balance matters because too much glutamate activity causes excitotoxicity (stroke, seizures, neurodegeneration), while too little impairs plasticity and learning.

**38. (Sections 2A, 1, 14)** Serotonin modulates mood, sleep, appetite, pain, and impulse control; too little is associated with low mood, anxiety, irritability, impulsivity, and sleep and appetite disturbance, but a simple "low serotonin" deficiency explanation is weakly supported, and antidepressants often take weeks to help. Dopamine supports movement, reward prediction, motivation, learning, attention, and working memory; too little is associated with slowed movement, tremor, and rigidity (Parkinson's disease), apathy, anhedonia, and poorer attention and working memory. Norepinephrine supports arousal, vigilance, attention, and stress responses; too little is associated with low alertness, poorer sustained attention, and low energy, and performance follows an inverted U. Reasons "too low" is not simple: signaling depends on synthesis, release, removal, receptors, and circuits rather than a single concentration; brain levels cannot be read from blood or urine; systems interact; evidence often comes from animals or from medication effects; effects differ by receptor and region; and common symptoms have many causes.

**39. (Sections 2A, 16)** Strong critiques include: brain levels are not reliably reflected in urine, blood, or saliva, and consumer neurotransmitter tests are not established as a basis for diagnosis or treatment; signaling depends on release, receptors, removal, and circuits, not just an amount; transmitter systems interact, so single-chemical stories are incomplete; "low neurotransmitters" is not a diagnosis and symptoms such as low mood, poor attention, or poor sleep have many causes; nothing shows that supplements marketed as "neurotransmitter support" correct a deficiency; too much can be as harmful as too little; and self-treatment can delay care. From the evaluation checklist, ask whether the claim rests on randomized, adequately powered, replicated human trials with appropriate controls, how large and durable the effect is, what adverse effects and interactions exist, and whether the claim confuses correlation with causation. Persistent or severe symptoms call for a qualified clinician.

**40. (Sections 2A, 2, 6)** Examples: dopamine, where loss of substantia nigra dopamine neurons causes slowed movement, tremor, and rigidity in Parkinson's disease; orexin, where loss of orexin neurons causes narcolepsy type 1 (excessive daytime sleepiness, sleep attacks, and cataplexy), with an autoimmune process suspected; vasopressin, where too little (central diabetes insipidus) causes large volumes of dilute urine, intense thirst, and a risk of dehydration; or acetylcholine, where loss of cholinergic basal forebrain neurons is a feature of Alzheimer's disease. These show that a specific signaling system can be necessary for a defined function and that losing it can produce a recognizable syndrome. They do not fully explain the disease: a vulnerable cell type or lost transmitter is a clue, not the whole mechanism, because genes, proteins, inflammation, blood vessels, circuits, and environment interact, and treatments that replace or boost a chemical (such as levodopa or symptomatic Alzheimer's medicines) generally address symptoms rather than the underlying degeneration.

**41. (Sections 2B, 6, 7)** Tau binds and stabilizes microtubules in axons and helps axonal transport; too little is thought to destabilize microtubules and impair transport, yet deleting tau in mice produces a mild phenotype, so disease is believed to involve both loss of normal function and a toxic gain (tangles). Huntingtin is a scaffold protein involved in vesicle and BDNF transport and gene regulation; complete loss is lethal in early development in mice, and Huntington's disease is caused mainly by a toxic gain from an expanded repeat, so therapies that lower huntingtin must avoid removing too much normal protein. Alpha-synuclein helps organize synaptic vesicles; mice lacking it show mild changes, while gene duplications cause familial Parkinson's disease, so too much is clearly harmful. Amyloid precursor protein supports neurite growth, synapse formation, and repair, and anti-amyloid therapies target aggregated amyloid-beta rather than normal APP. TDP-43 regulates RNA splicing; its nuclear loss causes splicing errors, including reduced stathmin-2. The distinction matters because toxic gain suggests reducing or clearing the harmful form, loss of function suggests replacing or preserving the protein, and a mixture means a narrow therapeutic window.

**42. (Sections 2B, 3, 4)** Examples with different mechanisms: autoantibodies removing NMDA receptors (anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis) cause memory loss, psychiatric symptoms, seizures, and reduced consciousness that can improve with immune treatment; loss of one working copy of SCN1A reduces firing of inhibitory interneurons and causes Dravet syndrome; loss-of-function variants in GABA-A receptor subunit genes such as GABRG2 are linked to epilepsy; GLUT1 deficiency limits glucose supply to the brain and causes infantile seizures, developmental delay, and low cerebrospinal fluid glucose, treatable in part with a ketogenic diet; and ATP1A3 pump variants cause alternating hemiplegia of childhood and rapid-onset dystonia–parkinsonism. Implications: GABA-A and NMDA receptors are major anesthetic and sedative targets, so molecular function bears directly on loss and recovery of responsiveness; receptor loss can alter awareness without destroying neurons; energy supply and excitability proteins underpin normal activity; and no single protein is a consciousness meter, so these findings do not settle the competing theories in the consciousness section.

**43. (Sections 2A, 14)** Examples: dopamine, where too little is associated with slowed movement, apathy, and poorer attention and working memory, and too much (usually from dopamine-raising drugs) with agitation, stereotyped movements, and psychosis-like symptoms, while too much dopaminergic medicine in Parkinson's disease can cause dyskinesias and compulsive behaviors; serotonin, where too little is associated with low mood, anxiety, impulsivity, and disturbed sleep and appetite, and too much with serotonin syndrome (agitation, tremor, twitching, sweating, fever), usually from combined serotonin-raising medicines; and acetylcholine, where too little is associated with attention and memory problems and loss of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons in Alzheimer's disease, and too much (from nerve agents or organophosphate pesticides) with confusion, seizures, coma, and a broad set of body effects. Excess usually comes from outside or from a broken off-switch because the brain tightly regulates its transmitters through feedback, reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, and receptor adjustment. The common causes of excess are therefore drugs, overdoses, poisons, or inherited defects in breakdown or removal (for example glycine buildup in nonketotic hyperglycinemia). "High" is no simpler than "low" because signaling depends on release, removal, receptors, and circuits rather than a single concentration; brain levels cannot be read from blood or urine; systems interact; effects differ by receptor and region (a transmitter may be excessive in one pathway and low in another); performance often follows an inverted U, so opposite states can produce similar symptoms; and the brain adapts, so prolonged excess produces tolerance and withdrawal rather than a stable "high" state.

**44. (Sections 2A, 16, 7)** Strong critiques include: "more serotonin is always better" is false, because for most transmitters performance follows an inverted U and excess is harmful; the symptoms described (jitteriness, sweating, twitching) fit the early features of serotonin syndrome, which includes agitation, tremor, muscle twitching or rigidity, sweating, diarrhea, fever, and fast heart rate, usually from combining serotonin-raising medicines, and can be life-threatening; nothing shows that supplements marketed as "neurotransmitter support" correct a deficiency or are safe to combine with prescription medicines; no blood or urine test reflects brain serotonin; "high serotonin" is not a diagnosis, and similar symptoms have many causes, so self-diagnosis is unreliable; and self-treatment can delay care. From the evaluation checklist, ask about the type of evidence, adverse effects and interactions, and whether the claim confuses correlation with causation. Symptoms such as high fever, muscle rigidity, confusion, or severe agitation after combining medicines or supplements, and slowed breathing or unresponsiveness after opioids or sedatives, call for urgent medical care (emergency services), and any questions about interactions belong with a clinician or pharmacist.

**45. (Sections 2B, 2C, 7)** Dosage sensitivity means that a gene or protein must stay within a window: too little and too much can each cause disease. Examples: MeCP2, where loss of function causes Rett syndrome and duplication causes MECP2 duplication syndrome (intellectual disability, floppy muscle tone, recurrent respiratory infections, seizures); PLP1, where mutations cause Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease and gene duplication is its most common cause; SCN2A, where gain-of-function variants tend to cause early-onset seizures and loss-of-function variants tend to cause autism or intellectual disability; FOXG1, where loss of one copy causes FOXG1 syndrome and duplications have also been reported; and APP and SNCA, where extra copies (as in Down syndrome and familial Parkinson's disease) cause disease. This complicates therapy because a treatment that raises or lowers a protein must land inside a narrow window and may need to differ by variant type. Huntingtin-lowering therapies must avoid removing too much normal protein, and a large huntingtin-lowering trial was stopped in 2021 after an unfavorable benefit–risk balance at the dose tested. Another example is that lowering a toxic protein with an antisense drug can help in one inherited form of ALS, yet the same strategy needs caution if the protein has normal functions. Opposite-direction variants in one gene, as with SCN2A, can also affect drug choice.

**46. (Sections 2C, 14, 16)** Strong critiques include: there is no "gene for" aggression, because most behavioral traits are influenced by thousands of variants of tiny individual effect plus environment; early candidate-gene findings often failed to replicate, as with 5-HTTLPR and depression, where large later analyses found no reliable support, and MAOA, whose "warrior gene" label overstates small, inconsistent effects of common variants (rare complete loss was reported in a few families); COMT and DRD4 findings are also small and inconsistent; heritability describes variation within a population and environment and is not determinism, since it does not mean a trait is fixed; penetrance and expressivity vary, and the same variant can cause severe disease in one person and little in another; pleiotropy and gene–environment interaction complicate any single-gene story; polygenic scores predict little for any one person and perform worse across ancestries; and from the evaluation checklist, ask about sample size, replication, effect size, and whether the claim confuses association with causation. A contrasting example of a large effect is a single-gene syndrome such as SHANK3, CHD8, SYNGAP1, or MECP2 loss, or a deletion such as 22q11.2, which raises the risk of psychosis in roughly one in four people in clinical samples, though most people with the deletion do not develop psychosis.

**47. (Sections 2C, 2B, 4)** Examples: patterning or migration, where LIS1 loss causes lissencephaly ("smooth brain") with severe intellectual disability and epilepsy, and DCX variants cause lissencephaly in males but a "double cortex" band of misplaced neurons in females because of X-inactivation; growth and size, where ASPM, WDR62, and related genes cause primary microcephaly by disrupting progenitor divisions, and PTEN or TSC1 and TSC2 loss disturbs mTOR signaling and causes macrocephaly, autism, tubers, and seizures; and synapse or circuit function, where SHANK3 loss causes delayed speech, intellectual disability, and autism features, SYNGAP1 loss causes intellectual disability and epilepsy with accelerated synapse maturation in mice, and loss of maternal UBE3A causes Angelman syndrome. The same variant can differ in outcome because of penetrance and expressivity, other genes, chance, somatic mosaicism (a variant present in only some cells), X-inactivation, imprinting, and environment. A genetic result needs a clinician and a genetic counselor because a variant is not destiny, interpretation (including variants of uncertain significance) is complex, results bear on family planning and treatment, and genetic information is sensitive (in the United States, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act does not cover life, disability, or long-term care insurance).

**48. (Sections 4A, 4, 16)** The three questions are: (1) Does the brain depend on quantum mechanics? Established, since atoms, bonds, protein structure, retinal photon absorption, drug binding, and MRI all follow quantum rules. (2) Do specific quantum effects play a functional role in neurons? Open: quantum effects are established or supported in other living systems (enzyme tunneling, photosynthesis, the bird compass), while evidence in neurons is limited, disputed, or absent, and proposals such as radical-pair chemistry or nuclear-spin (Posner) coherence remain speculative. (3) Is consciousness a quantum phenomenon? Speculative and contested: Orch OR is a minority view, and its indirect supports (such as a microtubule-stabilizing drug delaying loss of consciousness in rats, and tryptophan superradiance in a laboratory study) do not demonstrate it. Mixing the layers leads to overclaiming, such as moving from "the brain obeys quantum mechanics" to "consciousness is quantum." Quantum cognition uses the mathematics of quantum theory to model human judgments and does not claim the brain is physically quantum, so the success of those models is not evidence for a quantum brain.

**49. (Sections 4A, 16, 4, 15)** Strong critiques include: the claim slides between "the brain obeys quantum mechanics" (true of everything) and "neurons use quantum coherence" (unproven); the decoherence objection, in which Tegmark estimated about 10⁻¹³ seconds for microtubule superpositions compared with roughly 10⁻³ seconds for a neural action potential, and Hameroff's group's corrected estimate of about 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ seconds is longer but still short, with nuclear-spin proposals predicting far longer times that have not been directly measured in living brains; no experiment has directly measured coherence in living neural tissue; the supporting findings come from isolated molecules or animals (a microtubule-stabilizing drug delayed anesthetic unconsciousness in rats, and tryptophan superradiance was reported in vitro), which show that microtubules matter to anesthesia or that biology can sustain collective quantum effects in isolated structures, but not that the effect is quantum or that it produces consciousness; isotope-effect and MRI-entanglement reports need independent replication; a mysterious phenomenon plus a mysterious theory is not an explanation; a commercial product has no established evidence and the claim should be checked for testability, controls, effect size, and adverse effects; and even a confirmed quantum effect would leave the explanatory gap (why any physical process is accompanied by experience) open. The adversarial test of GNWT and IIT concerned classical-mechanism theories and does not address the quantum question directly.
