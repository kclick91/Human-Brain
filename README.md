# The Human Brain: What We Know, What We Debate, and What Comes Next

**Research brief \| Updated 13 October 2026**

> **Purpose and limits.** This document synthesizes mainstream
> neuroscience, major competing explanations, clinical interventions,
> and selected ongoing research. It is educational, not personal medical
> advice. Brain science changes quickly; clinical decisions should be
> made with qualified clinicians using current guidance. "Consciousness"
> is included throughout because it intersects with perception, memory,
> attention, sleep, anesthesia, mental health, and the ethical use of
> neurotechnology.

## Executive summary

The human brain is a living, adaptive information-processing organ
embedded in a body and environment. It contains neurons, glia, blood
vessels, immune-related cells, and complex chemical and electrical
signaling systems. No single region does all the work: most abilities
arise from interacting circuits whose contributions vary with task,
context, development, and state.

There is strong evidence that brain activity is necessary for ordinary
human perception, thought, emotion, memory, action, and wakeful
conscious experience. Damage, drugs, anesthesia, sleep, stimulation, and
disease can predictably change these functions. Yet neuroscience has not
fully explained **why and how physical activity is accompanied by
subjective experience**---the felt quality of seeing red, being in pain,
or having a thought. Researchers can identify neural correlates of
conscious states and test theories, but there is no universally
accepted, complete theory of consciousness.

The field is strongest at describing anatomy, cell types, circuits,
measurable signals, and many causal links between brain systems and
behavior. It is less complete at explaining high-level cognition from
first principles, predicting an individual's future mental state, and
turning discoveries into durable treatments for complex disorders. A
major modern strategy is to connect molecular cell atlases, wiring
diagrams, large-scale activity recordings, computational models, and
carefully controlled human studies.

Treatments range from sleep, rehabilitation, psychotherapy, and
medicines to surgery, implanted stimulation, noninvasive brain
stimulation, and experimental brain--computer interfaces. Every
intervention has a context-dependent benefit--risk balance. "Improving
the brain" is not one measurable goal: stronger attention, less anxiety,
better memory, creativity, emotional stability, and reduced pain may
trade off against one another.

## 1. What the brain is

The brain is the central organ of the nervous system. It works with the
spinal cord, peripheral nerves, endocrine system, immune system, sensory
organs, muscles, and internal organs. It is not an isolated computer:
its operation depends on oxygen, glucose, blood flow, body temperature,
hormones, sleep, and continuous feedback from the body.

### Neurons, glia, and synapses

-   **Neurons** receive, transform, and transmit signals. Dendrites and
    cell bodies integrate inputs; axons carry electrical impulses;
    synapses transmit signals to other cells.
-   **Action potentials** are rapid changes in membrane voltage that
    propagate along axons. Their timing and rate can encode information,
    but neural codes differ by circuit and task.
-   **Chemical synapses** release neurotransmitters that change the
    probability that a target cell will become active. Electrical
    synapses also exist.
-   **Excitation and inhibition** are both essential. Healthy function
    depends on their balance and timing, not simply on "more activity."
-   **Glial cells** are active participants, not mere packing material.
    Astrocytes support metabolism and regulate the extracellular
    environment; oligodendrocytes form myelin; microglia participate in
    immune surveillance and synapse remodeling.
-   **Neuromodulators** such as dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine,
    noradrenaline, and various peptides can change learning, motivation,
    arousal, and how circuits respond. It is misleading to reduce them
    to single labels such as "pleasure chemical."
-   **Plasticity** means that neural function and connections can change
    with experience, learning, injury, and development. Plasticity
    supports recovery and learning, but it can also reinforce
    maladaptive habits, chronic pain, addiction, or post-traumatic
    responses.

Consciousness relevance: conscious contents depend on neural activity,
but there is no simple equation such as "one neuron equals one thought."
Researchers investigate how distributed activity becomes coordinated,
how information is selected and maintained, and which activity patterns
distinguish conscious from unconscious processing.

## 2. How the brain works: a multilevel view

A useful model links several levels instead of choosing only one:

1.  **Molecules and genes:** receptors, ion channels, neurotransmitter
    synthesis, gene expression, and cellular energy metabolism constrain
    what cells can do.
2.  **Cells:** neurons and glia have diverse types and properties. Cell
    identity depends on molecular profile, anatomy, connections, and
    physiology.
3.  **Synapses and local circuits:** groups of cells transform signals
    through excitation, inhibition, recurrent loops, and plasticity.
4.  **Large-scale networks:** regions communicate through white matter
    and dynamic functional coupling. Network organization changes with
    task and brain state.
5.  **Cognition and behavior:** perception, planning, language, memory,
    emotion, and action emerge from coordinated biological processes.
6.  **Body and environment:** sensory input, movement, social context,
    hormones, sleep, learning, and culture shape brain function over
    time.

### Perception is inference, not a camera feed

Sensory systems transform physical energy into neural signals. The brain
combines these signals with context, attention, prior learning, and
expectations to construct useful interpretations. This often produces
stable, accurate perception, but it also creates illusions and
systematic errors.

**Predictive processing** is a family of models proposing that the brain
continually generates predictions and updates them using prediction
errors. It is influential and useful, but the claim that one predictive
principle explains all brain function is not settled. Competing and
complementary accounts emphasize feedforward feature processing,
recurrent processing, attention, active sensing, and task-specific
learning.

Consciousness relevance: a percept is not necessarily conscious just
because sensory information has been processed. Some processing occurs
without reportable awareness. Researchers debate which additional
operations---recurrent interactions, global availability, higher-order
representation, or other features---are required for conscious access.

### Attention, working memory, and decision-making

-   **Attention** prioritizes information or tasks; it can be driven by
    goals or by salient events.
-   **Working memory** temporarily maintains and manipulates
    information. It relies on distributed circuits and may involve
    persistent activity, short-term synaptic changes, or dynamic
    activity patterns depending on the task.
-   **Decision-making** combines evidence, goals, value, uncertainty,
    and constraints. It is not located in one "decision center."
-   **Executive control** helps maintain goals, suppress some responses,
    switch tasks, and monitor errors. These abilities are limited and
    depend on fatigue, stress, development, and context.

Consciousness relevance: attention and consciousness are related but not
identical. Something can influence behavior without being consciously
noticed, and people can sometimes report experience without strong
focused attention. How much attention is necessary for different forms
of consciousness remains disputed.

### Learning, memory, and identity

Memory is not one storage system. Working memory, episodic memory,
semantic knowledge, skills, habits, emotional learning, and conditioning
depend on partially distinct but interacting circuits. Memories are
reconstructive: retrieval can alter how an experience is later
remembered. Sleep contributes to memory processing, though the
mechanisms and benefits differ across memory types.

A person's identity depends on memory, bodily continuity, values, social
relationships, habits, and ongoing brain activity. No single memory
store or brain location fully contains a person's identity.

Consciousness relevance: the present moment can be conscious without an
accurate autobiographical memory of it. Conversely, memory reports can
be distorted. This complicates attempts to infer past conscious
experience from later testimony alone.

### Emotion, motivation, and the body

Emotion includes changes in attention, physiology, action readiness,
interpretation, and subjective feeling. The traditional idea that each
emotion has one dedicated brain location is too simple. Threat, reward,
sadness, anger, and social connection involve overlapping networks and
bodily processes.

Interoception---the sensing and interpretation of internal bodily
conditions---contributes to feelings of hunger, breathlessness, pain,
arousal, and emotion. The body can alter what a situation feels like and
how it is judged.

Consciousness relevance: a scientific explanation of emotion must
account for both measurable processes and felt experience. Brain imaging
can identify correlates of emotional states but cannot, by itself, read
a person's full subjective experience.

### Sleep and anesthesia

Sleep is an active biological state, not merely the brain "turning off."
Non-REM and REM sleep have distinct patterns; sleep supports health,
learning, emotional regulation, and metabolic functions. Dreams are
common in some sleep states, but vivid experience can occur in others
too.

General anesthesia changes brain dynamics and responsiveness through
drugs acting on multiple molecular targets and circuits. Different
anesthetics can produce unconsciousness through partly different
mechanisms. Loss of responsiveness is not always identical to absence of
all experience, and reports after emergence are imperfect evidence about
what occurred during anesthesia.

Consciousness relevance: sleep, anesthesia, coma, and disorders of
consciousness offer powerful---but ethically and methodologically
challenging---ways to study the conditions associated with conscious
experience.

## 3. Consciousness: what is known and what remains open

### Distinguish the questions

-   **Wakefulness/arousal:** Is the brain in a state that can support
    responsiveness?
-   **Awareness:** Is there experience or awareness of the environment
    or self?
-   **Conscious content:** What is being experienced---color, pain, a
    sound, a thought?
-   **Access consciousness:** Is information available for flexible
    reasoning, decision-making, and report?
-   **Self-consciousness:** Does experience include a representation of
    oneself, one's body, or one's thoughts?
-   **Phenomenal consciousness:** What does an experience feel like from
    the inside?

These questions overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A patient may
be awake but have severely impaired awareness; a person may have
experience without being able to communicate it; and reportability can
fail even when some processing remains.

### Broad areas of agreement

1.  Conscious states are systematically associated with brain states and
    are altered by lesions, seizures, anesthesia, sleep, psychoactive
    substances, and stimulation.
2.  Conscious contents are associated with activity in distributed
    systems, including sensory and association cortex, with recurrent
    interactions and state-dependent coordination.
3.  No single brain scan or behavioral test perfectly determines whether
    an uncommunicative person is conscious.
4.  Neural correlates are not automatically a complete explanation.
    Finding what reliably accompanies an experience does not by itself
    explain why that activity has a subjective quality.
5.  Strong tests require clear definitions, preregistered predictions,
    multiple measurement methods, and efforts to distinguish conscious
    experience from attention, memory, task demands, and reporting.

### A major recent test: GNWT versus IIT

A 2025 *Nature* study organized an adversarial collaboration between
proponents of GNWT and IIT. Researchers studied 256 participants across
fMRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial EEG. Results supported
some predictions of each theory but challenged key claims of both. In
particular, the expected sustained posterior synchronization for IIT was
not found, while some strong GNWT predictions about late "ignition" and
prefrontal representation were also challenged. The authors argued for
more rigorous, quantitative, theory-driven tests rather than declaring a
winner. [Cogitate Consortium et al., *Nature*
(2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08888-1).

**Interpretation:** the study is a meaningful advance, not proof that
consciousness has been solved or that either theory is entirely false.
It tested specific predictions in particular tasks and modalities; the
theories' advocates may revise biological implementations, and other
theories remain in play.

### Consciousness in medicine

Clinical assessment distinguishes level of arousal from evidence of
awareness. Disorders such as coma, unresponsive wakefulness syndrome,
and minimally conscious state require careful assessment over time. Some
patients who cannot follow commands may show brain responses consistent
with covert command-following on specialized tests. Such findings matter
for prognosis and communication, but no one method is infallible.

Consciousness is also central to pain, psychiatric care, anesthesia,
end-of-life decisions, and animal welfare. Ethical practice requires
humility: absence of outward response is not definitive proof of absence
of experience.

on the target condition, severity, individual biology, dose, duration,
access, and whether outcomes are measured in a meaningful way.
Treatments should be chosen with qualified clinicians, not
self-experimentation.

### Trade-offs in "brain enhancement"

-   **Performance versus well-being:** pushing alertness or work hours
    can degrade sleep, mood, learning, and health.
-   **Short-term benefit versus long-term uncertainty:** a drug or
    device may improve a task while having poorly understood long-term
    effects.
-   **Specificity versus generality:** interventions that improve one
    function may not improve unrelated abilities.
-   **Plasticity versus stability:** the ability to change supports
    learning but also makes harmful learning and maladaptive habits
    possible.
-   **Symptom relief versus underlying cause:** a treatment may improve
    function without stopping disease progression.
-   **Average effect versus individual response:** trial averages do not
    predict a particular person's outcome.
-   **Access versus innovation:** high costs, specialist availability,
    geography, and insurance rules determine who benefits.
-   **Measurement versus lived experience:** a change in a brain scan or
    test score may not translate into a meaningful improvement in daily
    life.

## 8. Technologies that may change neuroscience

### Better brain maps

Cell atlases and connectomes aim to identify what cell types exist,
where they are located, how they connect, and how they behave. A map is
foundational but incomplete: the same wiring can behave differently
depending on activity, neuromodulation, learning, and body state.

### Recording and manipulating activity

New electrodes, optical methods, molecular sensors, and noninvasive
imaging can record more cells or larger networks. Optogenetics and other
cell-targeted techniques are powerful in animal research, but
translating them safely and effectively to humans is a separate
challenge.

Consciousness relevance: the ability to perturb a circuit and observe a
change in perception or responsiveness can provide stronger causal
evidence than observation alone. Ethical constraints limit which
experiments can be performed in humans.

### Brain--computer interfaces

BCIs can translate selected neural patterns into cursor movements, text,
speech, or device commands. The goal is often restoration of
communication or movement, not unrestricted reading of thoughts. Systems
differ widely: some are invasive, some noninvasive, and many remain
research-stage.

Important limitations include signal drift, training burden, surgical
risks, data security, device maintenance, and unequal access. Neural
signals are context-dependent; a decoder trained for one task or person
may not generalize.

### NeuroAI and computational neuroscience

AI can help analyze high-dimensional neural data, segment microscopy
images, infer cell types, model circuits, and compare hypotheses.
Neuroscience can in turn inspire learning systems that adapt
efficiently, learn from limited experience, and operate under energy
constraints.

A model's success at predicting data does not prove that the brain uses
the same algorithm. Useful models should make risky, testable
predictions and be compared against simpler alternatives.

### Digital twins and personalized brain models

Researchers aspire to create individualized computational models of
anatomy, connectivity, physiology, and disease. Such models could
eventually aid surgical planning or treatment selection. At present, a
complete personal "brain simulation" capable of reproducing a person's
memories, personality, or consciousness is not an established clinical
capability.

## 9. Brain health: evidence-based foundations and limits

Broadly useful habits include adequate sleep, regular physical activity,
treatment of vascular risk factors, avoiding smoking and harmful
substance use, wearing appropriate head protection, maintaining social
and cognitive engagement, and seeking care for persistent mood, sleep,
memory, or neurological symptoms. Hearing and vision problems,
medication effects, and other medical conditions can also influence
cognitive performance.

These are risk-reduction and health-support measures, not guarantees
against disease. "Brain training" apps may improve practiced tasks, but
broad transfer to general intelligence or everyday functioning is much
less certain. Supplements marketed for memory or focus often have weaker
evidence than advertising suggests.

Consciousness relevance: healthy brain function supports wakefulness,
attention, emotional regulation, and the capacity to engage with
experience, but no lifestyle intervention has been shown to unlock a
hidden, unlimited level of consciousness.

## 10. Ethics, law, and society

### Mental privacy and neural data

Brain signals can reveal or permit inference about sensitive traits in
specific contexts. That does not mean current systems can freely read
arbitrary thoughts. Neurotechnology raises questions about consent, data
ownership, security, secondary use, and whether neural data deserve
special protections.

### Autonomy and enhancement

Brain stimulation, psychiatric medicines, and neuroprostheses can change
mood, behavior, motivation, or agency. Ethical use requires informed
consent, transparent discussion of uncertainty, respect for patient
goals, and monitoring for unwanted effects.

### Disability and personhood

BCIs and rehabilitation should be developed with disabled people rather
than merely for them. A person's worth is not measured by cognitive
performance, speech, movement, or technological "normalization."
Communication technology should expand agency and choice.

### Consciousness and moral status

Uncertainty about consciousness affects decisions involving infants,
severe brain injury, dementia, anesthesia, animals, and future
artificial systems. No simple behavioral checklist resolves all cases.
Evidence should be assessed carefully, and uncertainty should encourage
proportionate caution rather than confident speculation.

## 11. Persistent misconceptions

-   **"We use only 10% of our brains."** False. Many brain systems are
    active across ordinary activities, though not every neuron fires at
    once.
-   **"Left-brained people are logical; right-brained people are
    creative."** Oversimplified. Some functions are lateralized, but
    complex cognition uses both hemispheres.
-   **"Dopamine is the pleasure chemical."** Incomplete. Dopamine
    participates in learning, motivation, movement, and
    prediction-related processes.
-   **"Every memory is stored in one location."** False. Memory depends
    on distributed systems and can be reconstructed during retrieval.
-   **"Brain imaging can read minds."** Current decoding is limited,
    task-dependent, probabilistic, and often requires extensive training
    and cooperation.
-   **"A brain region has only one function."** False. Regions
    participate in multiple networks and functions.
-   **"More neural activity is always better."** False. Function depends
    on appropriate patterns, timing, inhibition, and coordination.
-   **"A treatment that changes a scan has necessarily helped."** False.
    Clinical benefit must be measured in meaningful outcomes, not only
    biomarkers.
-   **"Science has proved consciousness is an illusion."** No. There are
    theories about how the brain constructs perceptions and self-models,
    but subjective experience is a real phenomenon requiring
    explanation, even if interpretations of it differ.

## 12. The biggest open questions

1.  **The explanatory gap:** What complete account connects physical
    processes to subjective experience?
2.  **The neural basis of conscious content:** Which circuits and
    dynamics are necessary or sufficient for particular experiences?
3.  **Consciousness without report:** How can awareness be inferred when
    a person cannot communicate?
4.  **Causal circuit models:** How do cell types and synaptic rules
    produce flexible behavior across contexts?
5.  **Memory and identity:** How are memories stabilized, updated, and
    integrated into a changing self?
6.  **Individual differences:** Why do people with similar diagnoses or
    injuries respond differently to treatment?
7.  **Mental illness mechanisms:** How can research move from broad
    diagnostic categories to better mechanistic subtypes without
    reducing people to biomarkers?
8.  **Neurodegeneration:** How can treatment prevent or halt disease,
    not only modestly slow symptoms or progression?
9.  **Brain repair:** How can regeneration, plasticity, and
    neuroprosthetics restore function without harmful side effects?
10. **Ethical neurotechnology:** How can innovation protect privacy,
    autonomy, equity, and people whose ability to consent or communicate
    is limited?
11. **Cross-species inference:** Which findings from mice, primates, and
    artificial models generalize to human cognition and consciousness?
12. **What counts as explanation?** Can a theory predict and control
    brain behavior accurately while still leaving subjective experience
    unexplained?

## 13. How to evaluate future claims about the brain

When encountering a headline or product claim, ask:

1.  Is the evidence from cells, animals, a small human study, a
    randomized trial, or routine clinical practice?
2.  Was the study preregistered, adequately powered, independently
    replicated, and compared with an appropriate control?
3.  Is the outcome meaningful to people, or merely a change in an
    imaging signal or test score?
4.  How large is the effect, how long does it last, and who was studied?
5.  What adverse effects, uncertainty, costs, and access barriers exist?
6.  Does the claim distinguish correlation from causation?
7.  Are the authors clear about limitations and alternative
    explanations?
8.  Does the claim overreach from "associated with consciousness" to
    "explains consciousness"?

For consciousness theories specifically, look for predictions that
distinguish one theory from its rivals. A theory that can explain every
possible result after the fact is not a strong scientific theory.

## Final synthesis: where understanding stands

Neuroscience is not ignorant of the brain; it has accumulated a large
and reliable body of knowledge. Scientists can map many structures and
cell types, measure electrical and metabolic activity, identify circuits
involved in perception and action, establish causal effects of lesions
and stimulation, and treat a growing set of neurological and psychiatric
conditions. Modern brain research is increasingly quantitative,
collaborative, computational, and capable of connecting data across
scales.

But a detailed inventory is not the same as a complete explanation. We
do not yet have a finished account of how all these cells and circuits
generate human-level intelligence, emotion, personal identity, or
subjective experience. Even prominent consciousness theories face
empirical challenges, and the best recent head-to-head test did not
produce a decisive winner. Treatments can be powerful, but many are
partial, condition-specific, and accompanied by real costs and risks.
Emerging technologies---from cell atlases to BCIs---offer significant
promise, while also creating new ethical obligations.

The most accurate overall picture is **substantial progress without
final understanding**. We know much more about what the brain does,
which systems contribute, and how to measure and sometimes alter them
than we know about why experience feels the way it does or how to
predict every individual outcome. The path forward is not a single grand
theory or miracle technology: it is careful experiments, reproducible
data, causal tests, better models, inclusive clinical research, and
honest treatment of uncertainty. Consciousness is not a decorative side
topic in that effort; it is one of the clearest reminders of both the
power and the limits of current brain science.
